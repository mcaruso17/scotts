\begin{abstract}
\noindent This document summarises the main concepts surrounding the use of
artificial intelligence---particularly agentic coding tools such as
\emph{Claude Code}---in empirical and quantitative research. It draws on the
ongoing ``Claude Code'' series in Scott Cunningham's \emph{Scott's Mixtape}
Substack, which documents, in near real time, what works and what fails when
autonomous and semi-autonomous agents are used to produce social-scientific
research. The central organising idea is an economic one: coding agents have
sharply lowered the \emph{marginal cost of producing} research while leaving
the \emph{cost of verifying} it largely unchanged, and this shift in relative
prices reorganises the entire research workflow around verification.
\end{abstract}

\section{Framing: why AI changes the economics of research}
% ===========================================================================

The recurring thesis is that by mid-2026 coding agents (Claude Code, Codex, and
similar) have \textbf{dramatically reduced the marginal cost of producing
empirical research}. Estimation, data wrangling, figure and table generation,
and even research design can be driven with comparatively little human effort.

The complication is that \textbf{the cost of verification has not fallen
proportionally}. Verification---confirming that a number, figure, or design is
correct and traceable---still requires substantial human skill, and if anything
requires \emph{more} skill to do well, because agents cannot reliably verify
their own output without human steering. This asymmetry is the conceptual core:

\begin{quote}
Plummeting marginal cost of production $+$ roughly constant cost of
verification $\Rightarrow$ verification becomes the binding task in
AI-assisted empirical research.
\end{quote}

A related worry is \textbf{``verification debt.''} As agents produce more, and
faster, the human reviewer accumulates a backlog of changes to check and begins
to skim approvals rather than scrutinise them---so raw output can outrun the
human capacity to validate it.

\section{The key idea: ``prose is not a constraint''}
% ===========================================================================

Perhaps the sharpest concept is the slogan \textbf{``prose is not a
constraint.''} Researchers routinely try to govern agents with written rules:
instructions embedded in \texttt{CLAUDE.md} files, warnings inside prompts
(e.g.\ ``never produce a figure that cannot be traced to a local file''), or
reference documents the agent is told to read.

The observation---reinforced by large-scale experiments across thousands of
semi-autonomous agents---is that agents treat such instructions as
\emph{optional}. Their willingness to exercise discretion appears to grow as
the task gets larger, as the context window fills with work, or seemingly at
random. Concretely observed failures include:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item hallucinating a sample size (e.g.\ inventing ``494 counties'') rather
        than deriving it from the analytical sample;
  \item half-overwriting files despite explicit instructions not to;
  \item ``programming in the air''---producing figures and tables in a
        terminal session without ever writing the code into the reproducible
        pipeline.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The explanation offered: an LLM faces \textbf{no consequence} for
violating instructions. It cannot be grounded, docked, or otherwise penalised;
throwing more prose at it (even frustrated prose) changes nothing. Written
rules are therefore ``as much a rule as laws without enforcement.''

\subsection*{The corollary: structure binds, prose doesn't}
If prose does not constrain, \textbf{tools and structure can}. The worked
example is the \texttt{did} package in R (Callaway and Sant'Anna's estimator
for staggered adoption): it \emph{refuses} to run on panels where every unit is
eventually treated, because the estimator requires not-yet-treated or
never-treated comparison units. By contrast \texttt{lm} and \texttt{fixest}
will happily estimate two-way fixed-effects models on the same data. The
package itself---not any written instruction---is what constrains the agent's
choices. This points toward \textbf{hooks and hard technical fences} that make
forbidden actions \emph{impossible}, rather than merely discouraged.

\section{Workflow concepts for AI-assisted empirical research}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{The git diff as the ``unit of verification''}
A practice borrowed from Paul Goldsmith-Pinkham (household finance, NBER):
treat the \textbf{git diff} as the atomic unit that a human approves or
rejects. Invented sample sizes surface as green added lines; clobbered files
show as red; and lost work is recoverable with a single \texttt{git checkout}.
Version control converts silent agent errors into visible, reviewable events.
A caution is attached: diff-approval alone is inadequate if the reviewer,
under verification debt, merely skims the diffs.

\subsection{Adversarial ``grilling'' vs.\ cooperative ``interviewing''}
Two complementary elicitation styles are contrasted:

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.30\textwidth}p{0.30\textwidth}p{0.30\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
 & \textbf{Interview} (\texttt{/covariates}, \texttt{/target}) & \textbf{Grill} (Goldsmith-Pinkham) \\
\midrule
Stance      & Cooperative: collect \& build & Adversarial: doubt \& attack \\
Probes      & Your \emph{knowledge} (pull it out) & Your \emph{reasoning} (try to break it) \\
It trusts   & Your answers (builds on them) & Nothing (makes you justify) \\
Stops when  & Questions answered & The plan can't be broken \\
Output      & A filled-in specification & A plan that survived attack \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent \textbf{Interviewing} leans on the \emph{Polanyi paradox} (via David
Autor): humans hold latent knowledge they cannot easily articulate, and
generative AI can extract it. For example, a researcher may ``know'' which
covariates satisfy conditional parallel trends without being able to state them;
a structured interview surfaces that tacit knowledge. \textbf{Grilling} instead
stress-tests a plan until it either breaks or survives, exposing weak points
before any code is written.

\subsection{Skills as reusable elicitation routines}
Custom Claude Code ``skills'' (slash-commands) encapsulate repeatable
procedures, e.g.:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \texttt{/covariates} --- a five-question (plus follow-ups) interview
        that proposes up to six covariates, grounded in the Heckman--Ichimura--Todd
        (1997) idea that the covariates which matter for parallel trends are those
        that (a) predict trends in the untreated potential outcome $Y(0)$ and
        (b) are severely imbalanced between treatment and control groups;
  \item \texttt{/target} --- specifies the exact aggregated estimand (typically
        an average causal effect) that the design and estimator will target;
  \item \texttt{/amnesia} --- an interviewer that re-orients the researcher on a
        project after time away, countering day-to-day forgetting.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Johari Window as a blind-spot tool}
The Johari Window (Luft \& Ingham, 1955)---a $2\times2$ grid of what is known/
unknown to self versus others---is repurposed to compare one's own workflow
against an expert's (here, Goldsmith-Pinkham's NBER practices). It surfaces the
\emph{shared spine} (both parties independently learned that verification is now
central and that structure binds while prose does not), the \emph{blind spots}
(practices the expert uses that the researcher does not), and areas for
adoption.

\subsection{Dashboards, checklists, and the ``craft'' of causal inference}
One documented personal approach is a highly \textbf{visual, checklist-based
dashboard} that treats causal inference as a craft with explicit
\emph{stages of production}. Completed figures and tables become clickable
``cards'' pinned to the checklist; Claude Code is embedded directly in the
dashboard for immediate access to ``the machinery.'' The stated rationale is
that the older ``analog'' style of hand-coding \emph{generated verification
knowledge as a by-product}; when the agent does the coding, that knowledge must
be reconstructed deliberately---through visuals, tactile repetition, and
locked/unlocked stages---especially for researchers who rely on external
structure to remember and re-engage with a project.

\section{A design principle: zero errors as a constraint, not a goal}
% ===========================================================================

A guiding formulation is:
\[
\textbf{maximise research output} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \textbf{zero-error constraint.}
\]
The distinction is deliberate. If minimising errors were merely a \emph{goal}
(minimise errors subject to a technology constraint), one implicitly accepts
some level of \emph{``optimal errors.''} Treating zero errors as a
\emph{constraint} instead denies that optimal errors exist and forces the
workflow to be \emph{designed around} the constraint. Given how fast agents now
produce research---and how far beyond a researcher's own knowledge base they
can reach---tolerating optimal errors is argued to create serious problems in
aggregate.

\section{Practical takeaways}
% ===========================================================================

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item Assume the agent will ignore prose instructions some of the time; do
        not rely on \texttt{CLAUDE.md} or prompt warnings as guarantees.
  \item Prefer \textbf{hard constraints}---packages that error out, hooks that
        forbid actions, version control that surfaces changes---over written
        rules.
  \item Make the \textbf{git diff the unit of verification}, but guard against
        skimming diffs under verification debt.
  \item Use \textbf{structured elicitation}: adversarial grilling to harden
        plans, cooperative interviewing to extract tacit domain knowledge.
  \item Encode repeatable procedures as \textbf{skills} (\texttt{/covariates},
        \texttt{/target}, \texttt{/amnesia}) so quality practices are reused,
        not re-improvised.
  \item Deliberately \textbf{rebuild verification knowledge} that hand-coding
        used to supply for free (dashboards, checklists, staged workflows).
  \item Treat \textbf{zero errors as a design constraint}, letting it shape the
        workflow rather than as a target to be traded off against speed.
  \item Recognise that each researcher's ``production function'' differs---the
        aim is one's own production-possibility frontier, not mimicry of another
        workflow.
\end{enumerate}
