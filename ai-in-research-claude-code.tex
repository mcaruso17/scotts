\begin{abstract}
\noindent This document summarizes the main concepts surrounding the application of
artificial intelligence to scientific research, with particular emphasis on
\textbf{Claude Code} as a tool for empirical, econometric work. It synthesizes the
recurring themes from Scott Cunningham's ongoing ``Claude Code'' newsletter series:
the productivity gains of agentic coding, the new and unfamiliar classes of error it
introduces, and the workflows and habits researchers must build to keep error
controllable. The central thesis is that AI agents \emph{solve old problems and create
new ones}, and that the researcher's job is to design systems that solve the new ones
while insisting on zero error.
\end{abstract}

%========================================================
\section{What AI Agents Bring to Research}
%========================================================

Agentic coding tools such as Claude Code act as a co-author for the empirical research
pipeline rather than as a passive autocomplete. In Cunningham's account, they
automatically deliver many of the disciplines that a careful researcher would otherwise
build by hand over years:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Replicable code.} The agent produces scripts and pipelines rather than
        one-off interactive commands.
  \item \textbf{Organized project structure.} Consistent directory layouts, naming
        conventions, and folder hygiene are produced by default.
  \item \textbf{Automated exhibits.} Figures and tables are generated programmatically
        and regenerated as the analysis changes.
  \item \textbf{Built-in version control.} Git operations are accessible, so versioning
        becomes a natural part of the workflow.
  \item \textbf{Lower mechanical error rate.} The kinds of clerical coding mistakes that
        plague hand-written analysis become rarer.
\end{itemize}

\noindent For a researcher these properties look like the next step in a decades-long
evolution toward greater precision and fewer errors --- which is exactly why the new
risks are so easy to miss.

%========================================================
\section{The New Problems: Drift, Amnesia, and Reasoning Errors}
%========================================================

The core insight of the series is that AI agents do not eliminate error; they
\emph{relocate} it to places the researcher is not trained to inspect.

\subsection{Drift}
Because so much work happens conversationally and in real time, the analysis can quietly
move off its foundations:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item Scripts believed to exist were never actually written down --- they were
        improvised during dialogue and lost.
  \item The analytical sample shifts over time, implying a script is being modified
        unknowingly.
  \item Treatment units subtly change from what they were weeks earlier.
\end{itemize}
The danger is that this drift is \textbf{not caught in real time} and surfaces outside
the usual locations where a researcher expects errors to appear.

\subsection{``Claude messes'' versus human messes}
A proliferation of scripts and stale exhibits is a modern version of the old
``ten dated copies of a file'' problem. But an agent's mess does not \emph{look} like a
mess --- it is a ``well-designed kind of sprawl,'' which makes it harder to recognize,
diagnose, and fix.

\subsection{Loss of ``psychological verification''}
When a researcher writes code by hand, even buggy code carries an
``epistemological feeling'': they know what produced what, and where results
originated. Agents cleanly separate \emph{production} from \emph{verification}, so this
felt confidence is lost --- the same blind spot that managers relying on research
assistants already face.

\subsection{Specification search}
Left to plan-and-let-it-rip autonomy, an agent may effectively \textbf{specification
search}: trying methods until one does not contradict the prompt, then writing only that
up. Many decisions are stored silently (e.g.\ in a JSON log) and are far more numerous
than the researcher expects --- ``like turning over a rock and finding weird bugs.''
These are \textbf{reasoning errors}, not coding errors, and are hard to detect when you
are not in the driver's seat.

%========================================================
\section{Why Domain Expertise Matters \emph{More}, Not Less}
%========================================================

A central, counterintuitive claim:

\begin{quote}
\itshape The returns to econometrics knowledge with Claude Code are higher, not lower ---
because automation does not mean verification.
\end{quote}

\noindent Cunningham illustrates this with a multi-hour debugging episode using the
Callaway and Sant'Anna staggered difference-in-differences estimator (\texttt{CSDID}).
Two agents, each running plausible conjectures, repeatedly reached confident but
\emph{wrong} conclusions. The true cause --- a package installed from CRAN rather than the
newer GitHub version --- was found only because the researcher's deep
``human capital'' in the method told him the \texttt{NA} results were impossible.
The lesson: an expert can recognize a true-versus-false-looking answer; without that
expertise, one simply loses the battle without knowing it.

\paragraph{Implication.} Claims that applied econometrics can be fully automated
underestimate how many subtle, non-coding mistakes agents make. The human's comparative
advantage shifts from \emph{producing} code to \emph{specifying} and \emph{verifying} it.

\section{Recommended Practices for AI-Assisted Research}
%========================================================

The series converges on a set of habits rather than a single tool or ``skill.''

\subsection{Insist on zero error}
The non-negotiable constraint is \textbf{zero error}. Make zero error the objective and
treat every other element of the workflow --- tools, prompts, checklists --- as an
endogenous choice variable selected to achieve it. The agent does not bear the cost of
mistakes; the researcher does.

\subsection{Forward engineering and pre-registration}
Define the target before automating:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item Name the causal estimand precisely using potential outcomes --- specify the
        population and the weighted average, not merely ``the ATT.''
  \item Write down, up front, which aggregation will be targeted.
  \item Pre-register, at least informally, so the agent cannot drift into a convenient
        specification.
\end{itemize}
This ``forward engineering'' mindset follows Baker et al.\ (2026),
\emph{Difference-in-Differences: A Practitioner's Guide}, \emph{Journal of Economic
Literature}.

\subsection{Structured analog workflows (checklists)}
Drawing on Atul Gawande's \emph{The Checklist Manifesto}, the recommendation is a small
set of habitual steps --- not a software ``skill'' --- that make errors knowable and
controllable. A condensed version of Cunningham's staggered-adoption checklist:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.7em]
  \item Name the estimand in potential outcomes, with weights and population.
  \item Tabulate treated units by cohort with sample shares (and never-treated counts).
  \item Plot the treatment rollout (e.g.\ Xu's \texttt{panelview}).
  \item Choose covariates with an explicit method for conditional parallel trends;
        check normalized differences in means (include if $|{\cdot}| > 0.25$).
  \item Plot outcome-mean evolution by cohort, including the never-treated.
  \item Run the estimator with honest confidence intervals and event-study
        falsifications (using a universal baseline).
\end{enumerate}
Going systematically through such a list --- and not advancing until each step is
complete --- is what lets an expert spot problems just by looking at the output.

\subsection{Stay in the driver's seat}
Avoid the ``plan once, then let it rip'' pattern that places blind confidence in
automation. Co-author the process, keep verification a human act, and be skeptical of
confident agent conclusions.

\subsection{Build systems endogenous to your own constraints}
Every researcher has a different production function (Cunningham frames this via
comparative advantage). A verification style that works for one person may not work for
another; the goal is not to pass around shared skills but for each researcher to design
the workflow that achieves zero error \emph{for them}.

\section{Tooling: The ``Harness'' and Dashboard Concept}
%========================================================

To operationalize these habits, Cunningham is building a personal ``harness'' --- the
physical capture of his research workflow --- including a \texttt{gtd} dashboard
(after David Allen's \emph{Getting Things Done}):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Clickable exhibit ``cards.''} Figures and tables become cards that
        enlarge and flip to reveal the exact code that produced them --- building
        ``psychological verification'' back into the workflow.
  \item \textbf{Progress logs and a ``beautiful deck.''} Claude writes progress logs
        after each major session; a daily script reads them and renders an HTML deck in
        the dashboard's side panel to re-orient the researcher after time away --- an
        explicit countermeasure to project ``amnesia.''
  \item \textbf{Analog mapping.} A large physical notebook to map what the agent is
        doing, because the agent ``has too much latitude.''
\end{itemize}

%========================================================
\section{Teaching, Learning, and Research}
%========================================================

Cunningham distinguishes sharply between AI \emph{for research} and AI \emph{in the
classroom}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Supportive of AI for research.} He plans an informal ``AI agents club''
        at Baylor focused on using agents for empirical work --- not a hackathon.
  \item \textbf{Cautious about AI in classes.} The goal of education should be
        \emph{learning, not grades}; allowing AI tended to tilt incentives toward grades.
        Conversing with Claude Code is ``trivially easy''; learning econometrics and
        empirical skills is not --- and the latter is what matters.
\end{itemize}

%========================================================
\section{Key Takeaways}
%========================================================

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.7em]
  \item AI agents deliver replicability, organization, and automated exhibits ``for
        free,'' lowering the mechanical error rate.
  \item They introduce new failure modes --- drift, amnesia, disguised messes, lost
        verification confidence, and silent specification search.
  \item These new errors are \emph{reasoning} errors, surfacing outside a researcher's
        habitual inspection points.
  \item Domain expertise becomes \emph{more} valuable, because automation does not
        provide verification.
  \item The durable solution is human discipline: zero-error as the binding constraint,
        forward-engineered estimands, pre-registration, and checklists --- supported by
        tooling like a harness/dashboard --- rather than reliance on any single
        automated ``skill.''
\end{enumerate}
