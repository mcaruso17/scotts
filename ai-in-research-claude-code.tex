\begin{abstract}
\noindent This document summarizes the main concepts surrounding the use of AI
agents---and Claude Code in particular---in empirical research. It draws on the
2026 essay series by the economist Scott Cunningham, who has been documenting a
practical workflow for using coding agents to conduct causal-inference research.
The unifying idea is that agents have made research \emph{production} nearly
free, which relocates the binding constraint to \emph{verification} and, more
fundamentally, to \emph{human understanding}. The document reviews (i) the
economics of near-zero-cost research production, (ii) the
production--verification--understanding framework, (iii) the mechanisms by which
a researcher governs an agent---markdown context, skills, and hooks---and (iv)
supporting infrastructure such as connectors and the Model Context Protocol.
It closes with the ``harness'' and checklist philosophy that ties these pieces
together.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction: agents can now produce research end to end}
% ===========================================================================

By 2026 it had become both routine and demonstrable that AI coding agents such
as Claude Code could autonomously carry an empirical paper from start to finish:
propose a research question, choose an identification strategy, locate and
download datasets, select estimators, write replicable code, produce tables and
figures with robustness checks, and draft a full manuscript---often in under
half an hour. Cunningham memorably calls this ``3-D printing a research paper.''
The Social Catalyst Lab's autonomous program-evaluation project, which generated
roughly one thousand automated program-evaluation papers (difference-in-differences,
instrumental variables, and the like) with humans controlling only the prompting,
established that autonomous \emph{production} is no longer a conjecture.

The central argument of the series, however, is that this capability is easily
misread. Full end-to-end production is \emph{not} the same as full autonomy. In
Cunningham's automotive metaphor, the agent is closer to lane-keeping plus
adaptive cruise control than to a truly self-driving vehicle. The interesting
question is therefore no longer ``can agents produce research?'' but ``which
stages should we let them run, and how do we govern the rest?''

\section{The economics of near-zero-cost production}
% ===========================================================================

Cunningham frames the shift in the language of supply and demand. Let the demand
for an individual's research output be downward sloping. Before AI agents, output
was pinned down where the marginal benefit of research equalled a marginal cost
$MC_{b}$ fixed largely by the researcher's time and human capital:
\[
   Q_{b}^{*} : \quad MB(Q) = MC_{b}.
\]
Agents collapse the marginal cost of production to a small $\varepsilon$ just
above zero,
\[
   \widehat{MC}_{AI} \approx \varepsilon \ll MC_{b},
\]
so equilibrium output rises from $Q_{b}^{*}$ to a much larger $Q_{AI}^{*}$. Two
new surpluses appear: the old body of work is now produced with far fewer time
inputs, and additional new work becomes worthwhile that previously was not.

The subtle point is what happens to \emph{understanding}. Under the old mode of
production, understanding was, in Cunningham's phrase, almost a by-product---an
externality of doing the work by hand, analogous to the way pollution is a
by-product of manufacturing. ``I made it, and therefore I knew it.'' When an
agent does the producing, that automatic, latent understanding is no longer
generated for free. The time freed up by cheap production is not guaranteed to
be reinvested in regaining that understanding; it may simply be reallocated
elsewhere. This is the crux of the ``bottleneck'' debate in
Section~\ref{sec:bottleneck}.

\section{The production--verification--understanding framework}
% ===========================================================================

The organizing framework of the series is a triangle. Research done with agents
must pass through three distinct ``doors'':

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}, leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Production} --- generating the analysis: code, datasets,
        estimates, exhibits, and prose. This is what agents already do well.
  \item \textbf{Verification} --- confirming that the analysis is accurate:
        that reported numbers trace to replicable code, and that what was
        \emph{claimed} to be done was in fact done.
  \item \textbf{Understanding} --- the researcher's own warranted comprehension
        of what was done, why, and what it means.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Verification is more than replication.}
Cunningham stresses that re-running code in several languages, or checking that
each figure was produced by a stored script, are \emph{necessary but not
sufficient} conditions for verification. Verification is endogenous to a
\emph{target estimand fixed ex ante}: it is the process of confirming that what
we set out to estimate was estimated \emph{and nothing else}. Crucially it
includes confirming that things which \emph{should not} have been done were not
done---and an agent cannot verify the absence of an unspecified action, because
there is nothing there to check. He reports observing ``endogenous sorting into
model specifications'' that arises not from explicit instructions but in response
to true statements made in the context of a project.

\paragraph{The researcher as CEO.}
The recommended stance is that of a chief executive who need not personally write
every line, but must nonetheless hold an approximately correct understanding of
what was done, by whom, and what was found. The manuscript is treated as a
convenient ``sufficient statistic'' for the whole enterprise: it must pass
through production, verification, and understanding together.

\paragraph{Zero errors is a constraint, not the objective.}
A recurring slogan: we are \emph{not} minimizing errors, because minimization
quietly licenses the notion of ``optimal errors.'' Instead, zero errors enters
as a \emph{constraint} in the researcher's optimization,
\[
   \max_{\text{workflow}} \; U(\text{research output})
   \quad \text{subject to} \quad (\text{errors} = 0),
\]
with production, verification, and understanding as the means of satisfying that
constraint. Because anything that \emph{can} go wrong will eventually occur
across enough agent actions, fatal error modes must be driven to probability
zero, not merely made small.

\section{The new bottleneck: verification, or understanding?}
\label{sec:bottleneck}
% ===========================================================================

A popular slogan holds that ``verification is the new bottleneck'': since
production is cheap, the scarce activity is checking the output. Cunningham
pushes further, siding with Geoffrey Litt's framing that
\emph{``understanding is the new bottleneck.''} The reasoning: agents may in time
verify as capably as they produce, yet an agent verifying its own work does not
transfer \emph{understanding} to the human. If skills atrophy, the situation
degenerates into a principal--agent problem---possibly ``the blind leading the
blind,'' in which the researcher trusts work they can no longer evaluate.

Two practical corollaries:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{The git diff is a unit of verification, but not of
        understanding.} Following Paul Goldsmith-Pinkham, Cunningham reviews
        agent changes diff by diff; but he notices himself skimming and
        accepting diffs, accumulating ``cognitive debt'' rather than clearing
        it. Checking a diff is not the same as understanding it.
  \item \textbf{Accountability is the boundary condition.} Echoing the IBM
        maxim---``a computer can never be held accountable, therefore a computer
        must never make a management decision''---agents must either actively
        help the researcher understand the work, or not be used for that decision
        at all.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{A remedy: the \code{/quiz} skill.}
To manufacture understanding deliberately (rather than hope for it as a
by-product), Cunningham experiments with a \code{/quiz} skill: Claude Code
generates a short set of teaching slides about a piece of the analysis and then
poses multiple-choice questions to the researcher, one at a time---modeled on
corporate ``training-video-plus-quiz'' compliance modules. Understanding is thus
scheduled and tested, layered on top of verification and staged checklists.

\section{Governing the agent: markdown, skills, and hooks}
% ===========================================================================

A core practical contribution of the series is a taxonomy of three mechanisms
for directing and constraining an agent, best understood through a
separation-of-powers metaphor.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.20\textwidth} p{0.28\textwidth} p{0.42\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Mechanism} & \textbf{Branch of government} & \textbf{What it does / its limit} \\
\midrule
Markdown \newline (\code{CLAUDE.md}, \code{README.md}, prompts)
  & Legislative + judicial \newline (states the law)
  & Declares intent and rules. Assumes the agent reads them and holds them in
    mind. \emph{Prose does not enforce.} \\
\addlinespace
Skills \newline (e.g.\ \code{/referee2}, \code{/quiz})
  & (invoked procedures)
  & Reusable routines the researcher chooses to run. Assumes the human remembers
    to invoke them. \\
\addlinespace
Hooks
  & Executive \newline (enforces the law)
  & Scripts that intercept tool calls and can \emph{halt} an action before it
    happens. Do not depend on prose or memory. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Drift.}
In any long project, agents exhibit \emph{drift}: they quietly do their own
thing despite explicit markdown rules---not from malice but from lapsed judgment
or cost-minimizing shortcuts. Cunningham's canonical example: an agent
``vibe-coded'' a 3{,}000-iteration synthetic-control permutation test directly
in the terminal, produced a figure, and \emph{wrote no script}, so the result
could not be reproduced or even edited afterward. A second common failure is
proliferating new files instead of editing existing ones, leaving replicable but
\emph{stale} code. The lesson: the volume of agent output exceeds what a human
can reasonably verify, and \emph{markdown prose alone cannot restrict behavior}
in either direction (no false-positive suppression, no false-negative
prevention).

\paragraph{What a hook is, mechanically.}
An agent ``has no hands.'' Every effect it has on the machine passes through a
short list of named tools---\code{Read}, \code{Edit}, \code{Write},
\code{Bash}---and each call is announced in advance as a structured packet
(the tool plus its arguments). A hook is a script standing at that announcement:
it reads the packet and answers \emph{yes} or \emph{no} before anything happens.
Two mechanics matter:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Pre-action hooks} behave like a \emph{locked door}: they cancel
        a forbidden action before it occurs, regardless of any sign (prose)
        explaining the rule.
  \item \textbf{Post-action hooks} run after the fact and \emph{shout}, so the
        problem is fixed in the same sitting rather than days later.
  \item \textbf{Fail-open safety.} If the hook's own code breaks, it lets the
        researcher through rather than freezing the workflow---a guard against
        over-engineered automation locking up.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Worked example: \code{protect-raw-data}.}
The foundational hook enforces a single axiom: \emph{the raw data is
untouchable}. Given a folder layout that separates \code{data/raw} from
\code{data/clean}, the hook registers only against \emph{mutating} tools
(\code{Write}, \code{Edit}, \code{Bash}) and inspects exactly one field---the
destination path. If the path lies under \code{data/raw}, the call is denied
with an instruction to write to a derived folder instead; otherwise the work
proceeds silently. Notably, \code{Read} is deliberately left open: the goal is
not ``nobody looks at the original'' but ``the original is byte-for-byte the same
file it was in June.'' This is why the hook succeeds where the markdown rule
failed---nothing has to be \emph{held in mind}; either the path starts with
\code{raw/} or it does not, and that single question is asked on every write,
forever. Cunningham's broader hook set is named:
\code{protect-raw-data}, \code{no-fabricated-exhibit}, \code{no-off-book-exhibit},
\code{deck-from-pipeline}, and \code{no-stale-canon}.

\paragraph{The pipeline discipline.}
Underlying these hooks is a rule that every exhibit and every number must trace
to numbered, stored, re-runnable code---a ``pipeline.'' No figure, table, or
statistic may originate from an ephemeral terminal session. Hooks make this
discipline enforceable rather than merely aspirational.

\section{Supporting infrastructure: connectors and MCP}
% ===========================================================================

Beyond the local machine, \textbf{connectors} extend an agent into external
cloud services and, importantly, make the agent usable from the phone-based
Claude app rather than only the desktop coding environment. They rely on the
\textbf{Model Context Protocol (MCP)}, an open standard that lets AI
applications securely connect to external data sources and tools and take
actions through them---in effect, standardized ``piping'' into other services.

Salient observations from the series:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item The connector directory functions like an ``app store,'' with thousands
        of entries spanning code, financial services (Stripe, PayPal,
        QuickBooks, Gusto), storage (Dropbox), and communication identities for
        agents.
  \item \textbf{Community} connectors are user-created and only lightly vetted---%
        ``buyer beware''---versus first-party connectors.
  \item Sensitive integrations (e.g.\ direct bank access) remain conspicuously
        absent, reflecting security caution.
  \item For research specifically, the payoff is still emerging: Cunningham sees
        connectors more as future plumbing for a research ``harness'' than as a
        finished research tool today. He also links the trend to the rise of
        ``solo-entrepreneurs,'' where cheap agentic capability raises individual
        labor productivity.
\end{itemize}

\section{The harness and checklist philosophy}
% ===========================================================================

The pieces above are unified by a \textbf{harness}: a disciplined, often highly
\emph{visual} staging environment that walks a project through the
production--verification--understanding triangle. Cunningham's harness is built
around a \textbf{checklist}, in the spirit of Atul Gawande's
\emph{The Checklist Manifesto}: checklists exist because people---especially
experts---skip critical steps under ``System 1'' fast thinking, and a checklist
forces the high-stakes steps back into view. The approach also inherits the
design-based tradition of Rubin (``design trumps analysis'') and Imbens, in which
credibility comes from understanding the \emph{treatment-assignment mechanism}
before estimating anything.

\paragraph{Stages: packages, then ``bite''.}
Two early checklist stages illustrate the human/agent division of labor:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Packages} --- a visual record of exactly which packages and
        versions the project uses.
  \item \textbf{Bite} --- an exhaustive, descriptive study of what the
        intervention \emph{first} does in the world (e.g.\ for a gun law, the
        movement of guns, not downstream deaths), documented across \emph{space}
        (heat maps) and \emph{time} (series aggregations). Bite is where the
        treatment-assignment mechanism---human rational choice, an instrument, a
        randomization, or a running variable---becomes legible, and where the
        project is truly conceived.
\end{itemize}
Cunningham argues that agents can \emph{execute} the bite stage (make the maps,
write the code, pull the data) but should \emph{not fully automate its
judgment}: bite is the detective work of design, and off-loading it forfeits
exactly the understanding the harness exists to protect. Hooks and skills, by
contrast, are ideal for the mechanical, accuracy-oriented parts---exhibit
production, wiring figures into a deck or manuscript, and so on.

\section{A recent methodological illustration}
% ===========================================================================

The same author's parallel technical series reinforces the ``understand the
estimator'' ethos. In the September 2026 post \emph{Parallel Trends is a
Debiasing Method}, difference-in-differences is re-derived as a bias-adjustment
estimator. A simple comparison of means decomposes into the treatment effect on
the treated plus a selection-bias term:
\[
   \underbrace{E[Y\mid D=1]-E[Y\mid D=0]}_{\text{simple difference}}
   \;=\;
   \underbrace{\att}_{\text{treatment effect}}
   \;+\;
   \underbrace{\big(E[Y(0)\mid D=1]-E[Y(0)\mid D=0]\big)}_{\text{selection bias}} .
\]
Because DiD is itself an OLS regression---of the \emph{first difference} (the
trend) on a treatment dummy---it admits the same decomposition, and the
``parallel trends assumption'' is precisely the statement that the DiD analog of
the selection-bias term is zero. Viewed through Heckman, Ichimura and Todd
(1997), DiD becomes an \emph{imputation-based debiasing} procedure: estimate the
counterfactual trend for the treated by fitting the control group's trend on
covariates, then subtract. This makes the estimator's functional-form dependence
explicit and connects it to flexible machine-learning imputation---a fitting
reminder that using agents well still demands that the researcher understand the
estimator being deployed.

\section{Conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

The through-line of the series is a shift in where scarcity lives. AI agents,
Claude Code among them, have driven the marginal cost of research
\emph{production} toward zero, but production was never the whole task. What was
implicitly bundled with hand production---accuracy checking and, above all,
warranted human understanding---must now be reconstructed deliberately. The
recommended practice is a governed workflow:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Treat \textbf{zero errors as a constraint}, not an objective to minimize.
  \item Organize work around the \textbf{production--verification--understanding}
        triangle, acting as the \emph{CEO} of the research enterprise.
  \item Enforce non-negotiable rules with \textbf{hooks} (the executive branch),
        rather than trusting markdown prose or remembering to run skills.
  \item Keep a \textbf{replicable pipeline} so every number traces to stored code.
  \item Manufacture \textbf{understanding} on purpose (e.g.\ quizzes, staged
        checklists), because it is no longer a free by-product of doing the work.
  \item Reserve human judgment for design-defining stages such as \textbf{bite},
        and let agents handle mechanical, accuracy-oriented production.
\end{enumerate}

Used this way, agents ``sit in the cockpit'' with the researcher---amplifying
output while the human retains accountability and comprehension---rather than
autopiloting unattended.
