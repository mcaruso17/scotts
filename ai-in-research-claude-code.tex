\begin{abstract}
\noindent This document summarizes the main concepts around applying agentic
AI to research work, with an emphasis on \term{Claude Code}. It draws on the
practical, first-person experience reported in Scott Cunningham's
\emph{Scott's Mixtape Substack} (an economist adopting AI agents for empirical
causal-inference work) and on general practice for AI-assisted research. The
themes are organized as: (i) what agentic coding tools are; (ii) how they
extend to external systems via connectors and the Model Context Protocol
(MCP); (iii) the emerging discipline of a research ``harness''; and (iv) the
recurring cautions of \emph{production, verification, and understanding}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Why AI Agents in Research}

Traditional research computing has the researcher writing code directly in a
text editor, reading and seeing every line. \term{Agentic AI} tools invert
this: the researcher delegates implementation to a model that reads the
project, plans, edits files, runs code, and reports back. The promise is a
large increase in \term{labor productivity}---one person can accomplish what
previously required a team---while the risk is a loss of direct visibility
into what was actually done.

Cunningham frames the productivity gain in explicitly economic terms: as a
single worker produces more output ($Y/N$), that output feeds per-capita
income ($Y/\text{Pop}$) and, through market mechanisms, both growth (``the
wealth of nations'') and distribution (``the wages of nations''). A concrete
signal is the rise of \term{solo-entrepreneurs} (``solopreneurs'')---very
small firms, sometimes a single employee, an increasing share of which report
\$1--10M in annual revenue---a phenomenon partly enabled by AI tooling and
payment/back-office integrations.

\section{Claude Code: The Agentic Coding Environment}

\term{Claude Code} is a command-line agent that operates inside a project. Its
core research-relevant capabilities are:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \term{Whole-project context}: it reads the repository, not just a
        single snippet, so it can navigate a real analysis codebase.
  \item \term{Tool use}: it runs shell commands, executes scripts, edits
        files, and inspects outputs in a loop.
  \item \term{Reproducibility discipline}: good practice (echoed by
        Cunningham) is to \emph{never edit raw data} in place and to route all
        transformation through inspectable, re-runnable code.
  \item \term{Hooks}: user-defined scripts that fire on agent events (e.g.
        before/after a tool call) to enforce guardrails---formatting,
        validation, or protecting raw data---so the workflow encodes the
        researcher's standards automatically.
  \item \term{Availability beyond the laptop}: the same agentic capability is
        increasingly reachable from the Claude app (including on a phone) and
        from cloud environments, not only the local machine.
\end{itemize}

\section{Connectors and the Model Context Protocol (MCP)}

A central late-2026 theme is \term{connectors}: integrations that let the
assistant reach out to external data sources and services and take actions in
them. Key points:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \term{MCP (Model Context Protocol)} is the open standard underneath
        connectors---``a new kind of piping'' that lets an AI application
        securely connect to external tools and data and perform actions in
        real time.
  \item The connector directory functions like an ``app store,'' on the order
        of \emph{thousands} of entries and growing, spanning categories such
        as \emph{Code}, \emph{Financial Services}, and productivity tools.
  \item \term{First-party vs.\ Community connectors}: some are published by
        the vendor; \emph{Community} connectors are user-created and only
        lightly vetted---adopt them with a ``buyer beware'' posture,
        especially where credentials or sensitive data are involved.
  \item \term{Business/back-office reach}: connectors for Stripe, PayPal,
        QuickBooks, Gusto (payroll), and Dropbox make it feasible for one
        person to run substantial operations from the chat interface; banking
        APIs (e.g.\ direct bank access) remain conspicuously absent, plausibly
        for security reasons.
  \item \term{Research relevance is still emerging}: Cunningham is candid that
        the payoff of connectors for \emph{empirical research} (as opposed to
        running a small business) is not yet obvious to him, but he expects
        some cloud-only resources will eventually be reachable efficiently
        \emph{only} through a connector.
\end{itemize}

\section{The ``Harness'': Staging Research Cognition}

The most distinctive research concept in Cunningham's writing is the
\term{harness}---not a single tool but a structured \emph{staging of the
researcher's own cognition} so that projects move through disciplined stages.
Its design principles:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \term{Checklist philosophy}: each stage maps to a checklist. This
        mirrors the end-of-chapter ``Practical Tips / Practical Advice /
        Concluding Remarks'' distilled throughout his book \emph{Causal
        Inference: The Remix}, and the design-based tradition he credits to
        Don Rubin, Guido Imbens, and Pedro Sant'Anna.
  \item \term{Design principles for causal inference}: the harness is meant to
        \emph{discipline the mind}---forcing the analyst to state the
        identification strategy, covariate choices, and assumptions
        \emph{before} trusting an estimate.
  \item \term{Visualization to restore ``seeing''}: because delegating code to
        an agent removes the direct sight of one's own code, the harness
        re-introduces visual structure---interactive, ``flippable card''
        figures and a dashboard---so the researcher can remember and review
        what has been done. Cunningham even imagines mapping the harness into
        an Apple Vision Pro spatial interface.
  \item \term{Connector-supported, not connector-defined}: he expects the
        eventual harness to \emph{use} connectors heavily but to remain a
        graphical, workflow-centered environment of its own.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Governing Triad: Production, Verification, Understanding}

Cunningham reduces the goal of AI-assisted research to three requirements that
serve as a useful rubric for any researcher adopting these tools:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \term{Production}---actually generating the analysis (estimates,
        tables, figures) efficiently.
  \item \term{Verification}---confirming the output is what the researcher
        intended \emph{and} that it does what it claims to do (the estimator
        is correctly specified, the data pipeline is sound, results
        replicate).
  \item \term{Understanding}---maintaining a continuous, human grasp of what
        has been done, so the researcher is not merely accepting agent output
        on trust.
\end{enumerate}

The honest tension he reports---``drinking from a firehose'' and having to
``wrestle'' the agent to work on his terms---is itself an important concept:
raw agent speed is not the same as trustworthy research, and the whole point
of the harness and the triad is to convert capability into \emph{credible},
inspectable scholarship.

\section{Practical Takeaways for Researchers}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item Treat the agent as a fast collaborator, not an oracle: keep raw data
        immutable, route everything through re-runnable code, and read the
        diffs.
  \item Encode your standards mechanically (hooks, checklists) rather than
        relying on remembering them each session.
  \item Adopt connectors deliberately; prefer vetted ones and scrutinize
        Community connectors before granting access to sensitive systems.
  \item Build (or borrow) a lightweight ``harness'': explicit stages, a
        verification step, and a visual record of what was produced.
  \item Judge every workflow against production, verification, and
        understanding---if any one is missing, the result is not yet research.
\end{itemize}
