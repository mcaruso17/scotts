\begin{abstract}
\noindent This document summarizes the main concepts surrounding the use of AI
agents---and Claude Code in particular---in empirical and social-scientific
research. It draws on the ongoing ``Claude Code'' essay series (56+ essays since
November 2025) by economist Scott Cunningham (Baylor University), whose weekly
newsletter documents, in near-real-time, how AI agents are reshaping the
production of research. The framing throughout is economic: AI agents change the
\emph{production function} of cognitive output, and in doing so relocate the
binding constraint from production to \emph{verification}.
\end{abstract}

\section{What Claude Code Is}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
Claude Code is a command-line AI \emph{agent} built on Anthropic's Claude
models. Unlike a chat interface that only exchanges text, an agent operates
within an ecosystem of tools, memory stores, and orchestrated workflows,
enabling it to read and write files, run code, search the web, and act on
external systems (e.g., via the Model Context Protocol, MCP). For a researcher,
this turns the model from a conversational assistant into a collaborator that
can execute an entire empirical pipeline: cleaning data, estimating models,
producing tables and figures, drafting manuscripts, and building slide decks.

\section{The Central Economic Framework}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Production functions and the machine/human mix}
Consider the task of producing a single unit of \emph{cognitive output}---a
table of regression coefficients, a figure, a poem, or a worked-out manuscript.
That unit can be produced with different ratios of machine time $M$ to human
time $H$. Historically, before AI agents, the production of research was
extremely \textbf{human-time intensive}: machine time was little more than the
seconds Stata needed to invert a matrix.

Cunningham models this with an isoquant---a level surface of a quasi-concave
production function $Q = f(M, H)$:
\[
  \bar{Q} = f(M, H).
\]
\textbf{Before AI agents}, the isoquant was sharply convex and human-intensive.
\textbf{After AI agents}, it straightens toward linearity, approximating
\emph{perfect substitutes}:
\[
  Q = \alpha M + \beta H .
\]
When machine and human time become close substitutes, the cost-minimizing
choice is to push the input mix as far toward the machine-time intercept $M^{*}$
as possible---i.e., to let the agent produce as much as it can, as cheaply as it
can.

\subsection{Falling marginal cost of cognitive output}
The immediate consequence is that the \textbf{marginal cost of producing
cognitive output falls dramatically}. Research artifacts that once took days of
skilled human labor can be generated in minutes. This is the engine behind the
``research and publishing are now two different things'' theme in the essays:
when production is nearly free, the scarce, valuable activity shifts elsewhere.

\subsection{Production is not verification}
The pivotal idea in the series is that \textbf{the production function is not the
verification function}. AI agents collapse the cost of \emph{producing} output
but do nothing---directly---to reduce the cost of \emph{checking} that the output
is correct, or even that it is what the researcher intended. This opens a wedge:
\begin{quote}
\emph{``Verification is the new bottleneck.''}
\end{quote}
As production cost $\to 0$, the aggregate error rate can \emph{rise} unless
verification scales with output. More output produced faster means more
opportunities for silent, plausible-looking mistakes. The researcher's
comparative advantage migrates from \emph{writing} the analysis to
\emph{validating} it.

\section{The Manager / Individual-Contributor Shift}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
Cunningham borrows a distinction from the tech industry: there are
\textbf{managers} and \textbf{individual contributors}. AI agents push
researchers up the org chart. Using the metaphor of a Michelin-starred kitchen,
the founder (e.g., Paulo Airaudo of Amelia) no longer cooks every dish but is
still indispensable---without him there is no meal. By analogy, a principal
investigator such as Raj Chetty at Opportunity Insights directs a lab of research
assistants rather than writing every line of code.

With Claude Code, an \textbf{individual researcher becomes the manager of a lab
of AI research assistants}. Human capital reallocates from execution toward:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item posing the research question and forming conjectures;
  \item specifying tasks precisely enough for agents to execute;
  \item verifying, auditing, and interpreting results;
  \item exercising taste and judgment---the parts that remain irreducibly human.
\end{itemize}
A newcomer with a \$200/month subscription can now ``skip the line'' on
apprenticeship that once required years of moving up a hierarchy.

\section{The Human-in-the-Loop Problem and the ``Harness''}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
Because production has been externalized to agents, a new design problem
appears: \textbf{where does the human insert themselves into the new production
function?} Cunningham frames this as building a \emph{harness}.

\paragraph{Agent harness.}
An agent harness is the software infrastructure wrapping the large language model
that handles \emph{everything except the model itself}---``the complete
architectural system surrounding an LLM that manages the lifecycle of context:
from intent capture through specification, compilation, execution, verification,
and persistence.'' It is what connects the model to the outside world, lets it
use tools, and lets it remember information between steps.

The open research question the essays pursue is what an \emph{optimal harness for
empirical social science} looks like: how to record conjectures and
interpretations now that the human is further removed from typing code; how to
visualize ideas (dashboards, ``beautiful decks,'' markdown notes); and how to
draw the ``property rights'' boundary between researcher and agent over which
tasks. The philosophy is taken to precede the tooling---the essays start from a
productivity philosophy (e.g., David Allen's \emph{Getting Things Done}) and
treat the specific tools as endogenous to it.

\section{Concrete Workflows and Practices}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Skills}
A \emph{skill} is a packaged, reusable instruction set for a recurring task. The
early workflow was ``atomistic'': keep doing the normal task, but invoke a skill
to do it---\texttt{/beautiful\_deck} to build slides, \texttt{/referee2} to
check and validate code. Skills leave the researcher with something concrete and
copyable they can run on their own side.

\subsection{Multi-agent audits and replication}
A recurring, high-value pattern is using multiple agents to \textbf{audit and
replicate} empirical work: e.g., replication arcs of published papers, a
multi-agent audit of the Callaway--Sant'Anna difference-in-differences
estimator, and apples-to-apples comparisons across six packages implementing the
same estimator. These ``lab-notebook'' experiments let a single researcher take
on validation work that would previously have been impractical.

\subsection{Checklists to control error}
Because verification is the bottleneck, the essays argue that \textbf{applied
econometrics will increasingly require detailed checklists}---explicit,
enumerated validation steps that agents (and humans) run to drive the error rate
toward zero. Structured protocols substitute for the tacit checking that used to
happen implicitly while a human typed the code.

\subsection{Integration and measurement (MCP)}
Newer essays connect Claude Code to external platforms via MCP (for example,
Substack's own integration) and use agents to \emph{measure} outcomes---such as
estimating the ``social value'' of the essay series itself from subscriber and
readership data. This closes the loop: agents are used not only to do research
but to evaluate the research process.

\section{A Taxonomy of the Claude Code Essays}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
An agent-generated genre taxonomy of the 55+ essays identifies five types,
useful as a map of \emph{how} researchers engage with AI agents:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{Economic essays} (\raisebox{0pt}{$\sim$}19): argument-driven
        pieces on what AI does to research, publishing, and the profession
        (e.g., ``Zero Profit Condition,'' ``Verification Is the New Bottleneck,''
        ``Falling Marginal Costs'').
  \item \textbf{Workflow tutorials} ($\sim$16): practical, copyable how-to's
        (skills, deck prompts, harness principles).
  \item \textbf{Experiment / audit logs} ($\sim$13): lab-notebook write-ups of
        empirical results (replication and estimator-audit arcs).
  \item \textbf{Field notes} ($\sim$4): diary entries and talk recaps.
  \item \textbf{Artifact showcases} ($\sim$3): the output \emph{is} the post
        (videos, decks).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Key Takeaways}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item AI agents like Claude Code make machine time and human time near-perfect
        substitutes in producing cognitive output, driving its marginal cost
        toward zero.
  \item The binding constraint moves from \textbf{production} to
        \textbf{verification}; without scaling verification, aggregate error
        rises.
  \item Researchers become \textbf{managers of AI research assistants},
        reallocating human capital toward questions, specification, judgment, and
        auditing.
  \item Realizing the gains requires designing a \textbf{harness}---the
        human-in-the-loop infrastructure around the model---rather than merely
        collecting one-off tricks.
  \item Practical levers today: reusable \textbf{skills}, \textbf{multi-agent
        audits}, explicit \textbf{checklists}, and \textbf{MCP integrations} for
        acting on and measuring the research pipeline.
\end{itemize}
