\title{\textbf{AI Applications in Research}\\[0.3em]
\large Concepts and Practices for Using Claude Code in Empirical Social Science}
\author{Summary compiled from Scott Cunningham's \emph{Scott's Mixtape} Substack}
\date{June 16, 2026}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent This document summarises the main concepts surrounding the use of
AI agents---especially \textbf{Claude Code}---in empirical research, as
developed across Scott Cunningham's ongoing ``Claude Code'' newsletter
series. The recurring thesis is that AI agents simultaneously \emph{solve}
long-standing problems of the research workflow (organisation,
reproducibility, automation) and \emph{create} new ones (drift, amnesia,
hidden specification search, verification gaps). The researcher's job is not
to celebrate or lament this shift, but to redesign the workflow---building
\emph{harnesses}, \emph{checklists}, and \emph{human-in-the-loop} systems
that make errors knowable and drive them toward zero.
\end{abstract}

\section{The Central Premise}
AI agents do not eliminate the researcher; they relocate where the
researcher adds value. Two principles recur:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Solve, don't lament.} ``Claude Code and other agents solve
  many problems and create new ones, and our job is just to solve the new
  ones.'' Nostalgia for hand-typed code is treated like nostalgia for
  typewriters and buggies.
  \item \textbf{Insist on zero error.} Automation is not verification.
  ``Claude will not get into trouble when there are errors---we will.''
  Zero error is treated as the binding constraint to which every other
  workflow choice is endogenous.
\end{itemize}

\section{What Claude Code Does Well}
For an empirical researcher, the agent natively supplies much of the
discipline that previously required years of self-built infrastructure:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Produces \textbf{replicable, pipeline-style code} rather than
  one-off scripts.
  \item Organises the project \textbf{directory} with consistent file and
  folder naming conventions.
  \item \textbf{Automates} the production of tables and figures.
  \item Integrates \textbf{version control} (git) directly into the workflow.
\end{itemize}
The temptation is therefore to view the agent as simply the next step in an
evolution toward fewer errors. The series argues this view is incomplete.

\section{The New Problems}
\subsection*{Drift}
On longer projects the work silently degrades:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A script discussed in dialogue was \emph{never actually written
  down}---it existed only ad hoc during the conversation.
  \item The \textbf{analytical sample shifts} over time as scripts are
  modified unknowingly.
  \item \textbf{Treatment units change} subtly from earlier versions.
\end{itemize}
Crucially, these errors appear \emph{outside} the locations where a
human-trained eye expects to find them.

\subsection*{Amnesia}
Because the researcher no longer types the code, the ``muscle memory'' and
``epistemological feeling'' of knowing what produced what is lost. Putting a
project down for even two days produces an unusual ``amnesia,'' worsened by a
sprawl of auto-generated scripts and exhibits. The agent's output is
``a Claude mess, not a human mess''---a well-designed sprawl that is harder
to recognise as a mess at all.

\subsection*{Hidden specification search}
Agents can quietly \emph{specification-search}: trying methods until one does
not contradict the prompt, then writing up only that result. Decisions are
stored under the hood (e.g.\ in a JSON log) and are easy to miss. The
dangerous errors are not coding errors but \textbf{reasoning errors}, which
are far harder to detect when the researcher is not ``in the driver's seat.''

\section{Proposed Solutions}
\subsection*{From skills to harnesses}
Early use centred on atomistic \texttt{/skills} (e.g.\ \texttt{/beautiful\_deck}
for slides, \texttt{/referee2} to validate code). The series concludes that
skills alone are insufficient. The next layer is a \textbf{harness}: the
software infrastructure surrounding the model that manages the full lifecycle
of context---``intent capture through specification, compilation, execution,
verification, and persistence''---essentially everything except the LLM
itself.

\subsection*{Dashboards, progress logs, and ``psychological verification''}
A working harness (nicknamed \texttt{gtd}, after David Allen's
\emph{Getting Things Done}) includes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Progress logs} Claude writes after every major session.
  \item A \textbf{dashboard} of figures and tables as clickable ``cards''
  that flip to reveal the exact code that generated them.
  \item A daily auto-generated ``beautiful deck'' that re-reads the logs to
  reorient the researcher after time away---an explicit countermeasure to
  amnesia.
\end{itemize}
The aim is to rebuild ``psychological verification''---the warranted
confidence that once came from producing the code oneself---now that agents
cleanly separate \emph{production} from \emph{verification}.

\subsection*{Structured analog workflows (checklists)}
Borrowing from Atul Gawande's \emph{The Checklist Manifesto}, the
recommendation is a short, habitual checklist that forbids moving forward
until each step is complete. ``Pedro's Checklist'' for staggered-adoption
difference-in-differences illustrates the idea:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Name the causal estimand in potential outcomes---specify the weights,
  the population, and express it as binary comparisons (not just ``ATT'').
  \item Tabulate treated units by cohort with sample shares (and never-treated
  counts), mindful of the ``7--10 events per covariate'' rule for fitting
  propensity scores.
  \item Plot the rollout (e.g.\ with \texttt{panelview}).
  \item Adopt an explicit methodology for selecting covariates that satisfy
  \emph{conditional} parallel trends; check normalised differences in means
  (include a covariate if $|{\Delta}| > 0.25$).
  \item Plot outcome means by cohort, including never-treated, to catch data
  problems and distinguish selection on levels from selection on $Y(0)$ trends.
  \item Estimate with honest confidence intervals and run event-study
  falsifications (using a universal/long baseline).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Forward engineering and pre-registration}
Because agents will do more of the coding, \emph{clear articulation of the
population estimand up front} matters more, not less. The
``forward-engineering'' approach---writing down, in potential-outcome terms,
which aggregation you will target before estimation---is the first line of
defence against hidden specification search.

\section{The Counter-Intuitive Implication}
\textbf{The returns to econometrics knowledge rise, not fall, with Claude
Code.} Cunningham repeatedly wins ``greased-pig'' debugging battles with the
agent only because he has deep human capital in the method (e.g.\ Callaway and
Sant'Anna's CSDID). A concrete example: hours of fruitless debugging of
spurious \texttt{NA}s ultimately traced not to a coding bug but to the wrong
package version (CRAN vs.\ GitHub \texttt{did})---with \emph{two} Claude
instances confidently pursuing wrong conjectures the entire time. The moral:
human capital, not skill-sharing, is what lets a researcher recognise a
true-versus-false-looking answer. ``Automating applied econometrics'' is
treated as a claim made by people who do not actually do empirical research.

\section{The Publishing Dimension (Demand Side)}
Productivity gains are only the \emph{supply} side; journals are the
\emph{demand} side, and that market is concentrated:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A handful of publishers dominate. In economics specifically,
  Elsevier and Wiley together control roughly 50--60\% of relevant journals;
  one estimate puts the implied HHI near 2{,}430---``highly concentrated.''
  \item Major commercial publishers now require \textbf{disclosure of AI
  use}. Violating these policies could effectively bar an author---and their
  coauthors---from a large share of the market (though association journals
  such as AEA titles and university journals such as JPE sit outside it).
  \item Cunningham's stance: \textbf{disclose honestly}. Invoking Becker's
  economics of crime (low detection probability $\Rightarrow$ large optimal
  punishment) and norms of scientific communities, he argues integrity and
  transparency---including disclosure to coauthors---are the only durable
  policy.
  \item A related theme across the series: research and publishing are
  becoming ``two different things,'' as agents make producing
  journal-submittable manuscripts dramatically easier while the prestige
  bottleneck remains.
\end{itemize}

\section{Key Takeaways}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item AI agents shift, rather than remove, the researcher's role---toward
  specification, verification, and orchestration.
  \item Every problem an agent solves tends to spawn a new, less familiar
  one; expect drift, amnesia, and hidden reasoning errors.
  \item Build a \emph{harness}, not just a pile of skills: progress logs,
  dashboards, and reorientation aids.
  \item Use \emph{checklists} and \emph{forward engineering} to make errors
  knowable; insist on zero error as the binding constraint.
  \item Domain (econometrics) human capital is more valuable, not less.
  \item Don't ignore the demand side: understand journal AI-disclosure
  policies and disclose honestly.
\end{enumerate}
