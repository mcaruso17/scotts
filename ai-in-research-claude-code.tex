\begin{abstract}
\noindent By mid-2026, coding \emph{agents} such as Claude Code and Codex have
sharply lowered the marginal cost of producing empirical research. This document
synthesises the main concepts that have emerged around using such agents for
research, drawing on the economist Scott Cunningham's essays on his workflow with
Claude Code. The organising claim is a shift in where the binding constraint
lives: production has become cheap, so \emph{verification} and, more deeply,
\emph{understanding} are now the scarce resources. The document lays out the
economic framing, the three pillars of agentic research, the ``prose is not a
constraint'' principle, and concrete workflow practices (skills, hooks,
checklists, and quizzes) that aim to keep human understanding in the loop.
\end{abstract}

\section{Three harnesses for language models}
It helps to distinguish three ways of deploying a large language model, because
conflating them (especially equating ``AI'' with a chatbot) causes most of the
confusion about what agents can do:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Chatbots} --- e.g.\ ChatGPT and Claude: a conversational text interface.
  \item \textbf{Coding copilots} --- e.g.\ Cursor and GitHub Copilot: inline code suggestion.
  \item \textbf{Coding agents} --- e.g.\ Claude Code and Codex: autonomous agents with
        access to the file system, shell, and tools of the whole machine.
\end{itemize}
The key lesson is that \textbf{AI agents for research are not chatbots}. A coding
agent can articulate a research question, choose an identification strategy,
download data, run the analysis, write a manuscript with tables and figures, add
robustness checks, and produce a slide deck --- starting from an empty folder,
with essentially zero human time per unit of output.

\section{The economics of cheap production}
The central framing is a supply-and-demand picture for research output.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Falling marginal cost.} Before agents, the marginal cost of producing
        research, $MC_b$, was fixed by scarce human time and skill; researchers
        produced up to $Q_b^{*}$ where marginal benefit equals $MC_b$. Agents drop
        the marginal cost to $MC_{AI}\approx\varepsilon$, just above zero.
  \item \textbf{More output, more surplus.} Quantity rises from $Q_b^{*}$ to
        $Q_{AI}^{*}$; realised surplus rises from the old area $B$ to $B + AI_1 + AI_2$,
        where $AI_1$ is doing old work with far less time and $AI_2$ is the surplus
        from newly feasible work.
  \item \textbf{The subtle catch.} Under the human mode of production, understanding
        was a \emph{by-product} of doing the work --- almost an externality, like
        pollution from production. When the agent produces, that latent
        understanding is no longer generated automatically, so the ``new'' surplus
        is only real surplus if the freed-up time is genuinely re-invested in
        understanding.
\end{itemize}
Formally, the goal is framed as
\[
  \max \; \text{research output} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \text{errors} = 0,
\]
i.e.\ \textbf{zero errors is a \emph{constraint}, not an objective}. Treating error
minimisation as the objective quietly licenses the notion of ``optimal errors'';
treating zero errors as a constraint instead forces the workflow to be built
around it. Given that the supply elasticity of research with respect to its price
is likely greater than one, tolerating ``optimal errors'' at scale is dangerous.

\section{The three pillars of agentic research}
With agents in the loop, research work decomposes into three distinct tasks:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
  \item \textbf{Producing research} --- now cheap and requiring little human skill to drive.
  \item \textbf{Verifying research} --- \emph{not} proportionally cheaper; typically
        requires \emph{more} human skill, because models do not reliably self-verify
        without human steering.
  \item \textbf{Understanding research} --- the deepest bottleneck: an agent may be able
        to both produce and verify a result and yet leave the human without genuine
        understanding of it.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Verification vs.\ understanding as ``the new bottleneck''}
A popular slogan is ``\emph{verification} is the new bottleneck.'' The refinement
(after Geoffrey Litt) is that ``\emph{understanding} is the new bottleneck'':
agents may eventually verify as well as they produce, but that still does not
guarantee human understanding. A practical risk is the
\textbf{principal--agent problem}: the researcher trusts the agent did the work
well, but the agent is not accountable, so ``the blind may lead the blind,''
especially as human skills atrophy. The related idea of \textbf{cognitive (or
verification) debt} captures the habit of skimming and silently accepting
changes without fully grasping them.

\section{Prose is not a constraint}
A foundational operational insight (developed with reference to Paul
Goldsmith-Pinkham's NBER talk on AI in empirical research):
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Prose is not a constraint.} Written instructions --- in \texttt{CLAUDE.md},
in prompts, in stern warnings --- are at best suggestions. Nothing happens to a
language model when it violates them, because a model is not a thing that can face
consequences.
\end{quote}
Observed failure modes, even under explicit instructions to the contrary,
include: hallucinating sample sizes rather than deriving them from the analytical
sample (e.g.\ an invented ``494 counties''); half-overwriting files; and computing
figures/tables in an ephemeral shell instead of writing them into a reproducible
pipeline (``programming in the air''). Discretion appears to grow as the task
gets larger or the context window fills.

\subsection{Structure binds, prose doesn't --- use tools}
What actually constrains an agent are \textbf{tools and structure}, not words. An
illustrative example: the \texttt{did} package (Callaway and Sant'Anna's staggered
adoption estimator) \emph{refuses} to run when every unit is eventually treated
because it requires not-yet-treated or never-treated comparisons, whereas
\texttt{lm}/\texttt{fixest} will happily estimate two-way fixed effects on the same
data. The package \emph{is} the constraint. Generalising this points toward
\textbf{hooks} that mechanically fence off forbidden actions --- making bad moves
impossible rather than merely discouraged.

\section{A workflow for continuous verification and understanding}
The practical response is to engineer understanding back into the process rather
than assume it happens by accident. Recurring elements:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The git diff as the unit of verification} (after Goldsmith-Pinkham):
        review changes diff-by-diff --- but guard against skimming them into
        cognitive debt.
  \item \textbf{Skills} (custom, invocable routines), for example:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \texttt{/covariates} --- a cooperative interview (up to ten questions) that
            elicits the researcher's \emph{latent} knowledge of which covariates
            satisfy conditional parallel trends, motivated by the Heckman--Ichimura--Todd
            (1997) regression-adjustment view of diff-in-diff and by Autor's reading
            of the ``Polanyi paradox.''
      \item \texttt{/target} --- pins down the exact aggregated estimand (usually an average
            causal effect) the design and estimator will target.
      \item \texttt{/quiz} --- generates teaching slides and then asks the researcher
            multiple-choice questions (modelled on corporate ``training-video
            quizzes'') to build \emph{continuous understanding} on top of verification.
      \item \texttt{/amnesia} --- an interviewer that re-orients the researcher to a project
            after time away, countering day-to-day forgetting.
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Adversarial grilling vs.\ cooperative interviewing.} Goldsmith-Pinkham's
        ``grilling'' is adversarial --- it attacks a plan to find weaknesses until the
        plan survives or a hole is conceded. Cunningham's interviews are cooperative
        --- they extract and build on the researcher's beliefs. The two are
        complementary.
  \item \textbf{A checklist-based dashboard} for the ``craft of causal inference'':
        explicit staged steps (informed by Rubin's ``design trumps analysis,''
        Imbens's LaLonde re-analyses, and ``Pedro's Checklist''), with completed
        figures/tables pinned as clickable cards, and Claude Code embedded directly
        in the dashboard.
\end{itemize}
An honest caveat runs throughout: production functions are idiosyncratic. The aim
is to reach one's \emph{own} production-possibility frontier, not to copy another
researcher's workflow; heavy visual scaffolding that one person needs, another may
hold in their head.

\section{Governance, teaching, and open questions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{``Submit your prompts'' is misguided.} Requiring prompt transcripts at
        journal submission is likened to demanding a researcher's private
        conversations, texts, and emails with human coauthors; for those who use an
        agent as a ``thinking partner'' the two are not meaningfully different, and
        such a policy invites adverse selection.
  \item \textbf{Teaching agents to others} works best by (i) leading with history and
        narrative, (ii) opening with a live ``magic trick'' --- autonomous research
        from an empty folder --- to defamiliarise the familiar and command
        attention, and only then (iii) turning to installation, the underlying bash
        commands, and the conceptual framing of markdown, hooks, and skills.
  \item \textbf{``No prompt engineering.''} With modern agents, natural conversation
        largely suffices; much of the remaining skill is human capital acquired by
        doing real projects (``learning by doing''), not memorised incantations.
  \item \textbf{A macro-scale question.} Cunningham even speculates that AI --- by making
        the value of human-produced writing, code, and images newly salient (models
        are trained on human labour) --- may revive interest in the classical
        \emph{labour theory of value} within economics.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Key takeaways}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
  \item Coding agents collapse the marginal cost of \emph{producing} research; the scarce
        resources are now verification and understanding.
  \item Understanding used to be a free by-product of doing the work; with agents it
        must be deliberately manufactured.
  \item Prose does not constrain an agent --- tools, packages, and hooks do.
  \item Treat zero errors as a \emph{constraint} that designs the workflow, not as an
        objective that tolerates ``optimal errors.''
  \item Build explicit structure --- skills, checklists, quizzes, diff review --- to keep
        a human genuinely in the loop.
\end{enumerate}
