\begin{abstract}
\noindent This document summarizes the main concepts around the use of
artificial intelligence in empirical research, with a focus on the
agentic coding tool \emph{Claude Code}. The synthesis draws on the
economist Scott Cunningham's ongoing series of essays (60+ since
December 2025) documenting how AI agents are reshaping the day-to-day
craft of applied research. The recurring economic framing---falling
marginal costs, demand curves, diminishing returns, and workflow
(``harness'') design---is used to organize the ideas.
\end{abstract}

\section{Overview: AI as a Research Partner}

Modern coding agents such as Claude Code have moved beyond autocomplete
and debugging into the role of a genuine \emph{thinking partner} for
research. Rather than merely writing snippets, the agent reads context,
reasons out loud, executes multi-step empirical work, produces figures,
and communicates its reasoning back to the researcher in natural
language. The central claim across the literature is that this changes
not only \emph{how fast} research gets done, but \emph{which} research
becomes possible at all.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Ideation and scope.} Projects that would never have been
        started---because the setup cost was prohibitive---become
        feasible, expanding the researcher's opportunity set.
  \item \textbf{Depth.} The researcher can go deeper on empirical
        questions, iterating through more specifications, robustness
        checks, and visualizations than would otherwise be practical.
  \item \textbf{Joy and engagement.} A frequently reported effect is
        renewed enjoyment of the work itself, which sustains
        productivity.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Economics of AI-Assisted Research}

\subsection{Falling Marginal Cost of Production}
AI agents dramatically lower the marginal cost of producing research
output---code, tables, figures, and prose. When the price of an activity
falls, the quantity undertaken rises. This is why researchers using
Claude Code report a surge in output: more essays, more papers, more
analyses per unit of time.

\subsection{Output, Aggregate Error, and Quality Control}
Lower production cost is not a free lunch. As raw output rises, the
\emph{aggregate} number of errors can rise too, even if the error
\emph{rate} per unit falls. This makes verification, review discipline,
and human judgment more---not less---important. The bottleneck migrates
from \emph{producing} work to \emph{checking} it.

\subsection{Demand Curves and Diminishing Marginal Returns}
A demand curve is not just a description of a causal effect (raise
price $\rightarrow$ reduce quantity); underneath it lies a model of
humans valuing an activity. Marginal benefit is high when an activity is
done a little ($X_1$) and low when done a lot ($X_2$)---diminishing
marginal returns.

Applied to AI-assisted research, because the agent lowers the price of
work, the researcher does far \emph{more} of certain activities and
slides down the demand curve toward the low-value region. A concrete
example: the researcher must now \emph{read constantly}---the agent's
reasoning, its explanations, and its output---often more than ever
before. Past a point, reading suffers diminishing returns: the human
starts skimming, mindlessly approving diffs, and disengaging. The finite
human mind cannot absorb infinite words while maintaining comprehension.

\section{Designing the Workflow (the ``Harness'')}

A key insight is that when the researcher trips up, the right response is
often \emph{not} to ``work harder'' but to recognize a \emph{failed
design} and fix it. Building a personal ``harness'' that fits one's own
cognition---and repairs one's own broken production function---is central
to using these tools well.

\subsection{Concise, Warm Communication}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item Instruct the agent to be more succinct---``if I write 10
        characters, write 9''---while remaining warm and collaborative.
  \item Prefer terse, in-terminal communication, including ASCII
        graphics in the command-line interface, over long prose
        speeches.
  \item Codify durable preferences globally in a \texttt{CLAUDE.md} file
        (e.g., banning pet-peeve phrases) so they need not be repeated.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Oscillating Across Modalities}
To stay near the high-value part of the ``reading'' demand curve
($X_1$), deliberately rotate among modalities and mediums---text,
figures, checklists, and visual output---rather than consuming one
continuous stream of prose. Having the agent generate figures more
intensively and move through a well-designed checklist keeps the human
engaged and comprehending.

\subsection{Beware the Meta-Trap}
Time spent fixing designs is itself subject to a demand curve: one can
over-invest in tuning the workflow and reach the bottom of \emph{that}
curve. The recommended posture is balance---enough design work to
unblock real research, not so much that meta-work crowds out the work
itself.

\section{Tooling and Integrations}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Agentic execution.} Claude Code runs multi-step
        empirical work end to end---reading files, editing code, running
        analyses, and reporting results.
  \item \textbf{MCP integrations.} The Model Context Protocol (MCP) lets
        the agent connect to external platforms and services (for
        example, publishing integrations), extending its reach beyond
        the local codebase.
  \item \textbf{Figures over text.} Offloading communication to
        generated figures and ASCII visuals reduces the reading burden
        while preserving information.
  \item \textbf{Version-control discipline.} Reviewing commit
        ``diffs''---attentively rather than mindlessly---is the frontline
        of quality control in an agent-driven workflow.
\end{itemize}

\section{Key Takeaways}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item AI agents like Claude Code act as research \emph{partners}, not
        just code generators, expanding both the feasibility and depth
        of empirical work.
  \item By lowering the marginal cost of production, they raise output
        volume---and shift the binding constraint to human review and
        judgment.
  \item Economic intuition (demand curves, diminishing marginal returns)
        explains why more output can slide the researcher into low-value
        activity such as excessive, low-comprehension reading.
  \item The remedy is \emph{design}: build a personal harness---concise
        communication, \texttt{CLAUDE.md} conventions, checklists, and
        modality rotation---that keeps the human at the high-value part
        of the curve.
  \item Guard against over-optimizing the workflow itself; balance
        design effort against the research it is meant to enable.
\end{enumerate}
