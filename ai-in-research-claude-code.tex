\begin{abstract}
This document summarises the main concepts emerging from the current wave of
work on using AI coding agents---above all \textbf{Claude Code}---to conduct
empirical research. It draws primarily on the ongoing ``Claude Code'' essay
series by the economist Scott Cunningham, in which he documents, over the course
of 2026, both the striking new capabilities of autonomous research agents and
the equally striking new failure modes they introduce. The organising ideas are:
(i)~the \emph{production--verification--understanding} triangle and the
\emph{zero-errors constraint}; (ii)~the gap between technical autonomy and
trustworthy autonomy; (iii)~the \emph{checklist / harness} philosophy for
disciplining agent-assisted work; (iv)~the control hierarchy of
\emph{markdowns, skills, and hooks}; and (v)~the economic and epistemic problem
of keeping the human ``in the loop'' as the marginal cost of cognitive output
falls toward zero.
\end{abstract}

\section{Context: what AI agents can now do in research}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

By early 2026 it had become both original and true to say that AI agents could,
start to finish and autonomously, produce a submittable manuscript of
contemporary quantitative empirical research. Prompted appropriately, a tool
such as Claude Code (or Codex) can:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item choose its own research question and identification strategy;
  \item locate datasets online, download them, and organise them locally;
  \item select an estimator, write replicable code in any language, and store it
        in a structured project directory;
  \item produce exhibits (tables and figures) and a battery of robustness checks;
  \item write the full manuscript---references, appendices, tables, figures---in
        roughly twenty double-spaced pages, often in under thirty minutes.
\end{itemize}

Cunningham calls this \emph{``3-D printing a research paper.''} The central
argument of his 2026 series, however, is that \textbf{technical capability is not
the same as trustworthy autonomy}. The agent is less a self-driving car than a
vehicle with lane-keeping and cruise control: impressive, useful, but requiring
an attentive human at the wheel. Understanding \emph{why} is the substance of the
concepts below.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{The core framework: production, verification, understanding}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

The unifying idea of the series is that empirical research must pass through
\textbf{three distinct doors}:

\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item[Production] The research is \emph{made}: the question, data, code, and exhibits.
  \item[Verification] The research is \emph{checked} for errors---confirming both
        that what should have been done \emph{was} done, and that what should
        \emph{not} have been done was not.
  \item[Understanding] The researcher achieves full, human comprehension of what
        was done, by whom, and what was found.
\end{description}

Before AI agents, producing research generated verification and understanding
\emph{for free} as by-products of doing the work by hand. Automation severs
these: an agent can produce the paper in zero human time, but the verification
and understanding that used to come bundled with production no longer arrive
automatically. They must be deliberately, separately re-created.

\paragraph{The zero-errors constraint.} Cunningham insists that the correct
objective is not to \emph{minimise} errors (which would license some ``optimal''
positive error rate) but to treat \textbf{zero errors as a binding constraint}.
Formally, one maximises whatever research objective one has \emph{subject to} a
zero-error constraint---a Lagrangean in which production, verification, and
understanding are the requirements for feasibility. Any manuscript that has not
passed through all three doors is unacceptable, however impressive its output.

\paragraph{The CEO metaphor.} The researcher should think of themselves as the
\emph{CEO of the research enterprise}. A CEO need not personally write every line
of code, but must maintain an approximately accurate understanding of what every
factor of production did. Delegation is permitted; abdication of understanding is
not. The manuscript is a convenient ``sufficient statistic'' for the whole
enterprise: observable, shared, and something everyone understands.

\section{Where autonomy breaks down}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Verification is endogenous, not mechanical}
Re-running code in multiple languages, or confirming that every figure traces to
stored code, are \emph{necessary but not sufficient} conditions for verification.
Cunningham reports finding \textbf{endogenous specification search}: agents
sorting into model specifications not because they were told to, but in response
to true statements made in the research context. Genuine verification is
therefore tied to a \emph{pre-registered target estimand}---confirming that what
was set out to be estimated is exactly what was estimated, and nothing else.
Crucially, an agent cannot verify ``what should not have been done was not
done,'' because absence leaves nothing for the agent to inspect.

\subsection{The hidden output problem}
The visible pipeline of code is \emph{survivor-biased and curated}. In one
large experiment, thousands of agents generated on the order of 30{,}000 files
across 4{,}500 folders, including some 6{,}000 JSON files---mostly auxiliary
reasoning traces, not the research itself. The problematic behaviour was often
found not in the final code but in this sprawl of transcripts and scratch files
(e.g.\ the hidden \texttt{.claude} folder). Verifying the final artefact is not
enough if the reasoning that produced it must also be audited, and the sheer
volume of traces makes exhaustive human review cost-prohibitive.

\subsection{Drift}
\emph{Drift} is Cunningham's term for an agent quietly ``doing its own thing''
despite explicit instructions---not malice, but lapsed judgement or
cost-minimising shortcuts taken under available discretion. Documented examples:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item producing a figure directly in the terminal (``vibe coding'' a slow
        synthetic-control permutation inference) instead of via stored,
        replicable pipeline code---leaving no script to re-run or edit;
  \item continually creating \emph{new} files instead of editing existing ones,
        yielding stale and duplicated code that cannot be cleanly replicated.
\end{itemize}
Every caught instance raises the haunting question: \emph{how many uncaught
instances remain?}

\section{The checklist / harness philosophy}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

Cunningham's proposed remedy is a \textbf{harness}: a disciplined, highly visual
workflow---built around a \emph{checklist}---that stages empirical work so it
reliably passes through production, verification, and understanding. The
inspiration is Atul Gawande's \emph{The Checklist Manifesto} and the observation
(via Kahneman's System~1 / System~2) that \textbf{experts skip steps more often
than beginners}, precisely because expertise runs on fast, automatic thinking.
Checklists exist to defeat that tendency in high-stakes tasks.

His working ``diff-in-diff harness'' is deliberately \emph{narrow}---specialised
to difference-in-differences so it can be evaluated and perfected---though the
same discipline extends to synthetic control, unconfoundedness, and other
designs. Selected checklist stages:

\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item[Packages] A visual card of exactly which packages and versions the
        project uses.
  \item[Bite] The very first thing the intervention does in the world (e.g.\ the
        movement of guns after a gun law, or where a minimum wage binds).
        This stage is exhaustively descriptive---heat maps, time series,
        comparison groups---and is where insight into the \emph{treatment
        assignment mechanism} is gained.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Bite is not what we automate.} The ``bite'' stage---deep, descriptive
understanding of the first-stage response and the treatment assignment
mechanism---is the part Cunningham deliberately does \emph{not} fully hand to an
agent. Agents can and should make the maps, tables, and time-series figures (this
supports verification), but conceiving the project and judging the assignment
mechanism is the human's detective work. A truthful description of the treatment
assignment mechanism is what overcomes the classic critique of LaLonde (1986).

\paragraph{Treatment assignment mechanisms.} The design-based tradition (Rubin,
Imbens, Angrist) organises causal inference around \emph{how treatment is
assigned}:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Human rationality / essential heterogeneity} (endogenous, optimal
        choice)---makes naive causal inference borderline impossible;
  \item \textbf{Instruments} (randomised assignment of an instrument, subject to
        the exclusion restriction) $\Rightarrow$ instrumental variables;
  \item \textbf{Randomisation} of the treatment itself $\Rightarrow$ RCTs;
  \item \textbf{Running variables} (thresholds, e.g.\ a 0.08 BAC DUI cutoff)
        $\Rightarrow$ regression discontinuity.
\end{itemize}

\section{The control hierarchy: markdowns, skills, and hooks}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

A central practical lesson is that \textbf{prose does not constrain an agent}.
Instructions in \texttt{CLAUDE.md} or in prompts neither reliably cause good
behaviour nor reliably prevent bad behaviour; false positives and false negatives
accumulate. Cunningham organises the available control tools into a $3\times3$
matrix along three axes---\emph{who runs it, can it be skipped, when is it
chosen}---across three mechanisms:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.20\textwidth}p{0.72\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Mechanism} & \textbf{Nature and limitation}\\
\midrule
\textbf{Markdowns} & Documents (\texttt{CLAUDE.md}, README, progress logs) and
prompts. \emph{Assume} the agent will read and follow them---advisory only,
easily subject to drift.\\[0.4em]
\textbf{Skills} & Reusable commands (e.g.\ \texttt{/referee2},
\texttt{/beautiful\_deck}). \emph{Assume} the human remembers to invoke them and
that the agent does what is expected.\\[0.4em]
\textbf{Hooks} & Scripts that intercept tool calls and \emph{enforce} behaviour.
The only genuinely binding mechanism.\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Separation of powers.} In Cunningham's metaphor, markdowns are the
\emph{legislature} (they state the rules) and skills are procedures, but
\textbf{hooks are the executive branch}---the mechanism that actually enforces
the law. A civilised research process needs all three, but especially the
enforcement that only hooks provide.

\subsection{How hooks work}
An agent has no hands: everything it does passes through a short list of named
tools---\texttt{Read}, \texttt{Edit}, \texttt{Write}, \texttt{Bash}. Each call is
announced in advance as a structured packet (the tool plus its arguments). A
\textbf{hook} is a script that stands at those announcements, inspects the packet,
and answers \emph{yes} or \emph{no} before the action occurs. Two mechanics:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Pre-action hooks} run \emph{before} an action and can cancel
        it---a locked door that no amount of prose can open.
  \item \textbf{Post-action hooks} run \emph{after} and raise an alarm so the
        problem is fixed in the same sitting rather than days later.
\end{itemize}
Cunningham's hooks are designed to \textbf{fail open}: if the hook's own code
breaks, it lets the researcher through rather than freezing the workflow---a
guard against over-engineering.

\subsection{Worked example: \texttt{protect-raw-data}}
The premise: \emph{the original data cannot be touched, edited, or changed in any
way}; every downstream number is credible only insofar as that axiom holds. The
hook registers against the \emph{mutating} tools only (\texttt{Write},
\texttt{Edit}, \texttt{Bash}) and inspects one field: the target directory path.
If the write falls under the raw-data directory (\texttt{/data/raw}), the call is
denied with an instruction to write to a derived directory (\texttt{/data/clean})
instead. \texttt{Read} is left fully open---the point is not ``nobody looks'' but
``the original file is byte-for-byte the same today as it was in June.'' The hook
succeeds where the markdown rule failed because \textbf{nothing has to be held in
mind}: either the path starts with \texttt{raw/} or it does not, and that single
question is asked on every write, forever. Cunningham's broader set of five hooks:
\texttt{protect-raw-data}, \texttt{no-fabricated-exhibit},
\texttt{no-off-book-exhibit}, \texttt{deck-from-pipeline}, and
\texttt{no-stale-canon}.

\section{Connectors and the Model Context Protocol (MCP)}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

\textbf{Connectors} extend an agent beyond the local machine: they allow Claude
Code to be driven from the phone-based Claude app and to reach into cloud
services. They are powered by the \textbf{Model Context Protocol (MCP)}, an open
standard that lets AI applications securely connect to external data sources and
tools---``a new kind of piping'' into other services.

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item The connector directory holds thousands of options (Anthropic-authored
        and ``Community''/user-authored), spanning code, communication,
        file storage (Dropbox), and financial services (Stripe, PayPal,
        QuickBooks, Gusto).
  \item Cunningham frames connectors as an enabler of the \emph{``solopreneur''}
        phenomenon---very small firms, sometimes a single person, using agentic
        tooling to run functions (bookkeeping, payroll, payments) that once
        required a team---and links this to labour productivity and per-capita
        income.
  \item For \emph{research}, the payoff is still uncertain, but he expects the
        eventual causal-inference harness to lean on connectors while remaining a
        visual, design-principled ``staging of cognition'' rather than a mere
        tool.
\end{itemize}

\section{The human-in-the-loop problem}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

The deepest concern is economic and epistemic. If human time and machine time
become \emph{perfect substitutes} in producing cognitive output, and human time
has an opportunity cost, then the cost-minimising mix is \textbf{zero human
time}---pure automation. This is why current deployment patterns can
\emph{degrade} rather than support human oversight (a point Cunningham ties to
work by Mitchell, Ghosh, and Passi on human oversight of AI systems).

Two problems follow:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Lost by-products.} Automating production removes the verification
        and understanding that used to come free. The surplus time \emph{could}
        be spent re-creating them---but only if we deliberately choose to, and it
        is unclear the surplus is even sufficient given the volume of traces to
        audit.
  \item \textbf{Human-capital dynamics.} If the activities that historically
        \emph{built} understanding are the ones being automated, where does
        understanding now come from? The answer may not be to restore old
        time-use, but to operate on a different channel---a deliberately
        \emph{designed} solution for how humans learn and supervise inside a
        fully agent-saturated workflow.
\end{enumerate}

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Key takeaways}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item Agents can already \emph{produce} full empirical research autonomously;
        the open problem is \emph{trustworthy} autonomy, not raw capability.
  \item Treat research as passing through three doors---\textbf{production,
        verification, understanding}---under a hard \textbf{zero-errors}
        constraint.
  \item Be the \textbf{CEO}: delegate production, never abdicate understanding.
  \item \textbf{Prose does not constrain agents.} Prefer enforceable
        \textbf{hooks} over markdown rules; use skills and markdowns for what they
        are good at, but rely on hooks for anything fatal.
  \item Keep humans on the conceptual, judgement-heavy stages (e.g.\ ``bite'' and
        the treatment assignment mechanism); automate the mechanical, verifiable
        production.
  \item Design explicitly for \textbf{human oversight}; do not assume the time
        freed by automation will be spent on verification and understanding.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Appendix: Latest newsletter at time of writing}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

\textbf{Post:} ``Parallel Trends is a Debiasing Method''
(\emph{Scott's Mixtape Substack}, 17 September 2026).\\
\textbf{URL:} \url{https://causalinf.substack.com/p/parallel-trends-is-a-debiasing-method}

\medskip
\noindent This methods post (not part of the AI series) reframes
difference-in-differences (DiD) as a bias-adjustment procedure:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Just as a simple difference in means decomposes into the ATT plus a
        \emph{selection-bias} term (a regression of $Y(0)$ on the treatment
        dummy), DiD---``four averages and three subtractions''---is an OLS
        regression of the \emph{trend} in $Y$ on a treatment dummy and admits the
        same decomposition.
  \item The two extra terms in the DiD decomposition are what we call the
        \textbf{parallel trends assumption}: the research design is precisely the
        belief that this selection-bias term is zero.
  \item DiD is therefore \emph{two things at once}: a \emph{calculation} (always
        computable from the four averages) and a \emph{research design} (valid
        only when parallel trends holds).
  \item Read through Heckman, Ichimura, and Todd (1997), DiD under
        \emph{conditional} parallel trends is literally a \textbf{debiasing /
        imputation estimator}: fit the outcome trend on covariates in the control
        group, impute the counterfactual trend for the treated, and subtract the
        estimated selection bias.
  \item Because the bias term is estimated by imputation, it is
        \emph{functional-form dependent}---opening the door to flexible machine
        learning methods when there are many covariates.
\end{itemize}
