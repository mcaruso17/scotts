\begin{abstract}
\noindent This document summarizes the main concepts that have emerged around the use of
AI agents---and Claude Code in particular---in quantitative empirical research. It
organizes the material around a single framework, \emph{production, verification, and
understanding}, and connects it to the practical machinery of Claude Code: hooks,
skills, connectors, the Model Context Protocol (MCP), and diff-based review. The aim is
a compact, self-contained reference for researchers deciding \emph{which} parts of the
research pipeline to hand to an agent, and \emph{how} to keep human judgment in control
of the rest.
\end{abstract}

\section{The New Economics of Research Production}

The arrival of capable coding agents has sharply lowered the marginal cost of producing
empirical research. In simple economic terms, if a researcher historically produced
research up to the point where marginal benefit equaled a relatively high marginal cost
$MC_{b}$, agents push that marginal cost down toward a small $\varepsilon > 0$:
%
\[
MC_{b} \;\longrightarrow\; \widehat{MC}_{AI} \approx \varepsilon .
\]
%
Two things follow. First, the \emph{quantity} of research a single person can produce
rises substantially. Second, existing work is now produced with far fewer time inputs,
freeing capacity that can---in principle---be reallocated to other tasks.

The open question is whether the freed time actually restores the tacit
\emph{understanding} that used to be generated as a by-product of doing the work by hand.
This document argues, following current practice, that it does not do so automatically:
understanding must be deliberately engineered back into the workflow.

\section{Autonomous Production Is Real---but It Is Not Full Autonomy}

A useful, sobering observation: AI agents can already produce, start to finish, a
submittable empirical manuscript---choosing a question, an identification strategy,
locating and downloading data, selecting estimators, writing replicable code, generating
tables and figures, running robustness checks, and drafting a 20-page paper---often in
under an hour. This capability has been demonstrated at scale (for example, projects
generating on the order of a thousand automated program-evaluation papers using
difference-in-differences, instrumental variables, and related designs).

The important qualifier is that this is \emph{not} full self-driving research. The right
analogy is lane-keeping plus cruise control, not a truly autonomous vehicle: the agent
handles a great deal, but a human must remain the responsible driver. The reason is that
the hardest part of credible empirical work is not code generation but \emph{judgment}
about design, and judgment is exactly what cannot be safely delegated.

\section{The Core Framework: Production, Verification, Understanding}

The central organizing idea is that research must pass through three distinct
``doors'':

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.6em,style=nextline]
  \item[\textcolor{blue!55!black}{Production}] Generating the analysis: data, code,
  estimates, exhibits, and prose. This is the door agents open most easily and most
  fully.
  \item[\textcolor{blue!55!black}{Verification}] Confirming that what was intended was in
  fact done---\emph{and} that what should \emph{not} have been done was not done.
  \item[\textcolor{blue!55!black}{Understanding}] The researcher's own, non-delegable
  comprehension of what was done, why, and what it means.
\end{description}

\subsection{Verification is subtler than replication}
Re-running code in multiple languages, or confirming that every table and figure traces
to stored code, are \emph{necessary but not sufficient} conditions for verification. A
subtle failure mode is \emph{endogenous specification search}: an agent can drift into a
model specification in response to true statements made in the project context, rather
than any explicit instruction to do so. True verification is therefore relative to a
\emph{pre-specified target estimand}: it checks both that the intended analysis was
executed and that nothing off-target crept in. The latter---confirming the absence of
what was never supposed to happen---cannot be delegated to the agent, because from the
agent's point of view there is nothing to verify.

\subsection{Understanding is the real bottleneck}
A popular slogan holds that ``verification is the new bottleneck.'' A sharper statement
is that \textbf{understanding is the new bottleneck}. Agents may eventually verify as
well as they produce, but that still would not guarantee that the human understands the
result. Historically, understanding was almost an \emph{externality} of production---a
by-product created accidentally by the act of doing the work. When production is
outsourced, that by-product disappears unless it is intentionally rebuilt. The
governing principle (often attributed to IBM) is that since a machine cannot be held
accountable, it cannot be trusted to make the decision: agents must either help the human
understand the work, or not be used for it.

\subsection{Zero errors as a constraint, not an objective}
A recurring discipline: treat \emph{zero errors} as a hard constraint, not as something
to be minimized. Framing it as minimization quietly licenses ``optimal errors,'' which is
unacceptable when the elasticity of research supply is high. In Lagrangean terms, one
maximizes research value \emph{subject to} production, verification, and understanding all
being satisfied, with zero errors imposed as a binding constraint.

\section{Checklists and ``Harnesses''}

Because experts skip steps---often \emph{more} than beginners, a consequence of fast,
System-1 thinking---high-stakes work benefits from explicit checklists (in the spirit of
Atul Gawande's \emph{The Checklist Manifesto} and Daniel Kahneman's
\emph{Thinking, Fast and Slow}). In empirical practice, checklists are already implicit in
the design-based tradition (e.g., ``design trumps analysis''), where credible inference
depends on understanding the \emph{treatment assignment mechanism} before estimating
anything.

A \textbf{harness} extends the checklist into software: a visual staging environment that
routes a project through the production--verification--understanding stages. A worked
example is a difference-in-differences harness whose early ``bite'' stage exhaustively
documents the first-stage, boots-on-the-ground response to an intervention (maps, time
series, comparison groups) \emph{before} any estimation. Notably, this conception stage
is the part one should \emph{least} automate: agents can draw the maps and write the code,
but the researcher must own the detective work of establishing how, where, and to whom the
treatment actually reached the world.

\section{Claude Code in Practice}

Several concrete Claude Code features map directly onto the framework above.

\subsection{Hooks}
Hooks let the researcher wire deterministic, repeatable actions into the agent's workflow
(for example, standardized figure and table production, or a guardrail such as
``never edit raw data''). Hooks are best made \emph{specific} to each checklist stage
rather than abstract and universal---they are the mechanism by which verification steps are
enforced automatically.

\subsection{Skills}
Skills are reusable, invokable routines. A representative example is a \texttt{/quiz}
skill built explicitly to defend \emph{understanding}: Claude Code generates teaching
slides on the current analysis and then asks the researcher multiple-choice questions
about it---modeled, only half-jokingly, on corporate ``training-video quizzes.'' The point
is to force continuous comprehension on top of verification.

\subsection{The git diff as the unit of verification}
A common review practice is to treat each git diff as the atomic unit of verification.
This is valuable, but carries a warning: skimming and rubber-stamping diffs accumulates
\emph{cognitive debt}. Checking off a diff is supposed to clear that debt; in practice it
is easy to accept changes without genuine understanding. Diff review should therefore be
paired with an active understanding practice (such as the \texttt{/quiz} idea above),
so the goal becomes two things---verification \emph{and} understanding---not one.

\subsection{Connectors and the Model Context Protocol (MCP)}
\emph{Connectors} extend Claude beyond the local machine into external cloud services, and
work not only in Claude Code but also in the phone-based Claude app. They are powered by
the \textbf{Model Context Protocol (MCP)}, an open standard that lets AI applications
securely connect to external data sources and tools to fetch real-time information and take
actions---effectively the ``piping'' between the model and outside systems. The connector
directory functions like an app store (on the order of thousands of entries) and mixes
first-party connectors with lightly-vetted \emph{community} ones, so a ``buyer beware''
posture is warranted. For research, connectors are promising but still exploratory; their
clearest near-term value is convenience (e.g., searching cloud file storage from the
phone) and, more speculatively, reaching cloud resources that are only efficiently
accessible through an MCP connection.

\section{Keeping the Human in the Loop}

The recurring worry is a principal--agent problem: the researcher trusts that the agent did
the work well, but the agent is not a real accountable party, and if the researcher's own
skills atrophy, they may lose even the ability to judge whether the work \emph{was} done
well---``the blind leading the blind.'' The mitigations that follow from the framework are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Decide \emph{ex ante} which stages are automated and which are reserved for human
  judgment (design and the ``bite''/conception stage are prime candidates to keep manual).
  \item Encode verification as hooks so it happens by construction, not by memory.
  \item Add an explicit understanding practice (quizzes, interviews, teaching slides) so
  comprehension is produced on purpose rather than left to chance.
  \item Hold zero errors as a constraint and a pre-registered target estimand as the anchor
  for verification.
\end{itemize}

\section{A Practical Workflow Checklist}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{State the target estimand} and the design before touching data; write it
  down so verification has an anchor.
  \item \textbf{Pin the environment} (packages and versions) so results are reproducible.
  \item \textbf{Do the ``bite'' work by hand} (or closely supervised): document the
  first-stage, real-world response to the intervention exhaustively.
  \item \textbf{Automate production} of exhibits and robustness checks via hooks and
  skills, keeping raw data immutable.
  \item \textbf{Verify against the estimand}, checking both that intended steps ran and
  that no off-target specification search occurred.
  \item \textbf{Review diffs actively}, not passively; refuse to accept changes you cannot
  explain.
  \item \textbf{Quiz yourself} on the analysis to convert verification into durable
  understanding.
  \item \textbf{Treat any error as a stop condition}---zero errors is the constraint.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

AI agents have made research \emph{production} nearly free at the margin, and Claude Code
supplies concrete tools---hooks, skills, connectors, and diff review---to industrialize
it. But cheap production shifts the binding constraint downstream to
\emph{verification} and, most of all, to \emph{understanding}, which no longer arrives for
free as a by-product of doing the work. The practical response is to build a disciplined
harness: automate production, enforce verification mechanically, and deliberately
manufacture understanding, all under a hard zero-error constraint. Used this way, agents
sit in the cockpit \emph{with} the researcher rather than flying the plane while the
researcher sleeps.
