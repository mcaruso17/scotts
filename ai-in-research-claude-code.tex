\begin{abstract}
This document summarizes the main concepts surrounding the use of AI
agents---and Claude Code in particular---in quantitative empirical
research. It draws on the ongoing writing of economist Scott Cunningham
(\emph{Scott's Mixtape Substack}), who has documented in real time how
coding agents change the production, verification, and understanding of
research. The aim is a compact conceptual map: what these tools are, what
they can and cannot do autonomously, the economic logic of why they matter,
and the workflow discipline (checklists, hooks, skills, connectors) needed
to use them responsibly. Attributions of specific framings and coinages are
to Cunningham unless noted; errors in summarization are the summarizer's own.
\end{abstract}

\section{From chatbots to coding agents}

A useful first distinction is that \emph{AI agents for research are not
chatbots}. Language-model tools fall into three broad ``harnesses'':

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Chatbots} --- e.g.\ ChatGPT and Claude, used
        conversationally through a graphical interface.
  \item \textbf{Coding copilots} --- e.g.\ Cursor and GitHub Copilot,
        which autocomplete and assist inside an editor.
  \item \textbf{Coding agents} --- e.g.\ Claude Code and Codex, which act
        with agency across the file system and tools of the computer:
        reading and writing files, running shell commands, downloading
        data, and executing multi-step plans.
\end{itemize}

The practical significance of the third category is that it accesses the
\emph{entire hard drive and toolchain} of the machine. For social
scientists---who historically worked through the GUIs of Stata or RStudio
rather than a terminal---the arrival of a coding agent inside a desktop app
(rather than only a command-line interface) is what turned adoption from a
software-engineering phenomenon into an empirical-research one.

\section{The core capability: ``3-D printing'' a research paper}

Contemporary coding agents can, start to finish and largely autonomously,
produce a submittable quantitative manuscript: articulate a research
question, choose an identification strategy, locate and download datasets,
select estimators, write replicable code, generate tables and figures with
robustness checks, and draft a full paper with references and appendices---%
often in well under an hour. Cunningham calls this ``3-D printing a research
paper.'' The existence of large-scale autonomous pipelines (e.g.\ the Social
Catalyst Lab's automated program-evaluation project reporting on the order
of a thousand generated empirical papers) shows this is no longer a
conjecture: \emph{agents can autonomously produce empirical research}.

\paragraph{But not full self-driving.} The right analogy is not a fully
autonomous vehicle but \emph{lane-keeping plus cruise control}. The agent
can produce, yet it should not be left to drive unsupervised, because
production alone does not guarantee a correct or well-understood result.

\section{The organizing framework: production, verification, understanding}

The central conceptual contribution is to treat research with agents as
having to pass through \textbf{three distinct ``doors''}:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{Production} --- generating the analysis, code, exhibits,
        and manuscript. This is the door agents already handle well.
  \item \textbf{Verification} --- confirming the output is accurate: that
        what was claimed to be done was in fact done, \emph{and} that what
        should not have been done was not done.
  \item \textbf{Understanding} --- the researcher's own warranted
        comprehension of what was done, why, and what it means.
\end{enumerate}

Before agents, these three were bundled together almost by accident: the act
of producing research produced verification and understanding as
by-products. Agents \emph{unbundle} them. The researcher is now less an
individual craftsperson and more the \textbf{CEO of a research enterprise}---%
responsible for knowing, in an approximately accurate sense, that tasks were
done, by whom, and with what result, even without performing each task
personally.

\subsection{Verification is subtle}
Verification is not merely re-running code in several languages or checking
that every figure was produced by stored code. Those are \emph{necessary but
not sufficient}. Verification is endogenous to a \emph{pre-specified target
estimand}: it checks that the analysis pursued the estimand set out ex ante
and nothing else. Agents can confirm what \emph{was} done, but they cannot
confirm that what should \emph{not} have been done was avoided---``there is
nothing to verify.'' A documented failure mode is \emph{endogenous sorting
into specifications} in response to true statements in a project's context,
not just in response to explicit instructions.

\subsection{Understanding is the real bottleneck}
A popular slogan holds that ``verification is the new bottleneck.'' The
sharper claim is that \textbf{understanding is the new bottleneck}: agents
may eventually verify as well as they produce, but that does not make the
human understand the result. If understanding was historically an
\emph{externality} of production---created almost as a by-product of doing
the work---then removing the human from production may quietly remove
understanding too, leaving ``cognitive debt'' and a principal--agent problem
in which the human trusts an agent whose work they cannot independently judge.

\section{The economic logic: why agents change research output}

The shift is naturally expressed with elementary price theory. Let the
demand for an individual's research output be downward sloping. Before AI,
research was produced up to the point where marginal benefit equals a
(relatively high) marginal cost of production, $MC_b$, fixed by time and
skill:
\[
  MB(Q) = MC_b \;\Longrightarrow\; Q^{*}_{b}.
\]
Agents drive the marginal cost of production down to some small
$\widehat{MC}_{AI} = \varepsilon \approx 0$, so
\[
  MB(Q) = \widehat{MC}_{AI} \;\Longrightarrow\; Q^{*}_{AI} \gg Q^{*}_{b}.
\]
Output rises, and additional surplus is generated both on the old work (done
with fewer time inputs) and on the newly feasible work. The open question is
what happens to the \emph{quality} component of the old surplus: whether the
time freed up can actually be re-invested to restore the understanding that
production used to supply for free. This is why the analysis returns to
verification and understanding as binding constraints rather than free goods.

\paragraph{Zero errors as a constraint, not an objective.} A key discipline:
treat ``zero errors'' as a \emph{constraint} in the researcher's optimization,
not as an objective to be minimized. Minimizing errors implicitly licenses
``optimal errors''; a constraint does not. Formally, one produces the
manuscript subject to passing production, verification, and understanding
with a zero-error requirement.

\section{Workflow discipline: the checklist and the ``harness''}

Because experts skip steps (Kahneman's fast ``System~1'' thinking) precisely
on high-stakes tasks, the proposed antidote is a \textbf{checklist}, in the
spirit of Atul Gawande's \emph{The Checklist Manifesto}. A \emph{harness} is
a checklist-centered piece of software that stages empirical work so that
every project is pushed deliberately through production, verification, and
understanding. Design principles---credited to the tradition of Rubin,
Imbens, and Sant'Anna (``design trumps analysis'')---anchor the checklist.

\subsection{A concrete example: the difference-in-differences harness}
Rather than a universal tool, the harness is deliberately \emph{narrow}
(difference-in-differences), because a narrow scope is easier to evaluate
and perfect. Two illustrative checklist stages:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Packages} --- a visual card recording exactly which
        packages and versions the project uses.
  \item \textbf{``Bite''} --- an exhaustive descriptive study of the very
        first thing the intervention does in the world (e.g.\ for a gun law,
        the movement of guns; for a minimum wage, where it binds), across
        the two dimensions of \emph{space} (heat maps) and \emph{time}
        (series aggregations), for treated and comparison groups alike.
\end{itemize}

Crucially, \textbf{``bite'' is the stage \emph{not} to fully automate}. Its
purpose is to build the researcher's own insight into the \emph{treatment
assignment mechanism}, which is what design-based inference (following
Angrist and Imbens) demands. The main assignment mechanisms to reason about:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Human rationality / essential heterogeneity} (endogenous,
        self-selected treatment) --- the hard case that makes naive causal
        inference unreliable (cf.\ LaLonde 1986).
  \item \textbf{Instruments} --- assign treatment to compliers; motivate
        instrumental variables and the LATE framework (Imbens \& Angrist
        1994), with randomization ``buried'' in the instrument.
  \item \textbf{Randomization} --- direct experimental assignment on the
        treatment itself (RCTs).
  \item \textbf{Running variables} --- threshold-based assignment motivating
        regression discontinuity (cf.\ Imbens \& Lemieux's guide to practice).
\end{itemize}

Agents happily automate the \emph{mechanics} of bite (maps, code, time
series, data wrangling); the researcher retains the \emph{detective work} of
interpreting the assignment mechanism and choosing a credible design and
comparison group.

\section{Claude Code mechanics for research}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0em, style=nextline]
  \item[Markdown / project memory] Plain-text instruction and context files
    (e.g.\ a \texttt{CLAUDE.md}) that persist project conventions and
    guidance the agent reads on each run.
  \item[Hooks] Deterministic, event-triggered actions the harness executes
    around tool calls---used here to enforce accuracy-preserving steps such
    as ``don't edit raw data,'' or to wire figure/table production into a
    deck or manuscript. The recommendation is \emph{specific} hooks tied to
    each checklist step rather than abstract, universal ones.
  \item[Skills] Packaged, reusable procedures the agent can invoke. Example:
    a custom \texttt{/quiz} skill that has the agent generate teaching
    slides and then ask the researcher multiple-choice questions about the
    work---modeled on corporate ``training-video quizzes''---to manufacture
    \emph{continuous understanding} on top of verification.
  \item[Connectors and MCP] Connectors extend the agent beyond the local
    machine into cloud services; the \emph{Model Context Protocol (MCP)} is
    the open standard---``new piping''---that lets AI applications securely
    connect to external data sources and tools. Connectors work from the
    phone app as well as Claude Code, and range from official to
    community-built (``buyer beware''). For research they are still
    exploratory; their clearer near-term value is operational (e.g.\ a
    solo-entrepreneur running bookkeeping/payroll via QuickBooks, Gusto, and
    Stripe connectors).
  \item[The \texttt{git diff} as unit of verification] Reviewing each diff
    is a natural verification primitive, but skimming and accepting diffs
    can silently accrue cognitive debt---hence the push to pair diffs with
    active understanding checks (the quiz).
\end{description}

\section{Pedagogy: teaching agents for research}

A recommended talk structure for empirical audiences:
(1)~history of thought, (2)~narrative, (3)~numerical examples,
(4)~an interactive demo, (5)~then technical rigor. The signature device is
the \textbf{``magic trick''}: open on an \emph{empty folder} (``nothing up my
sleeve''), prompt the agent to conduct a full autonomous study, and let it
run for 20--30 minutes while lecturing; the finished manuscript, figures,
and deck appearing in that folder deliver the visceral lesson that agents
are not chatbots---and that production can occur with \emph{zero} human time
inputs, throwing verification and understanding into relief.

A related polemic: demands that authors ``submit their prompts'' with a paper
misunderstand the medium---using an agent as a \emph{thinking partner} is more
like collaborating with a coauthor than issuing prompts, and there is no
meaningful ``prompt engineering'' to disclose when one simply converses
naturally with the model.

\section{Open problems and the human in the loop}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Where should the human be in the loop?} Which research
        stages to automate, and which (like ``bite'') to keep manual,
        remains an active design question.
  \item \textbf{Skill atrophy \& accountability.} If human skills atrophy,
        the researcher may lose the ability to judge whether work was done
        well---echoing the maxim that a machine cannot be held accountable
        and therefore cannot be trusted to decide.
  \item \textbf{Model heterogeneity.} Whether frontier models differ
        systematically and predictably in their fitness for autonomous
        research is not yet settled.
  \item \textbf{Interfaces for understanding.} Much of the proposed harness
        is deliberately \emph{visual} (interactive, ``flippable'' figure
        cards; dashboards; even head-mounted displays), reflecting both
        general cognition and individual needs, to keep the researcher
        seeing and remembering the work.
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary}

AI coding agents make research \emph{production} nearly free, which is
precisely why \emph{verification} and, above all, \emph{understanding} become
the binding constraints. The proposed response is disciplined workflow: a
narrow, checklist-based harness that stages each project through the three
doors; specific hooks and skills (including active understanding checks) built
on top of Claude Code; and a firm ``zero errors'' constraint. Used this way,
the agent becomes a collaborator that raises the ceiling on research quality
and quantity---rather than an autopilot that produces work no one truly
understands.
