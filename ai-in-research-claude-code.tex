\begin{abstract}
\noindent This document summarizes the main concepts behind the use of
artificial-intelligence agents in academic and applied research, with an
emphasis on agentic coding assistants such as Anthropic's \emph{Claude Code}.
It organizes the emerging practice around a simple framework---the separation
of research \emph{production}, \emph{verification}, and \emph{understanding}---
and illustrates each with concrete workflows now common in empirical social
science: reproducibility audits, multi-language pipeline replication,
autonomous empirical paper generation, and communication of results. The
summary draws on public writing about agentic research, most directly the
ongoing ``Claude Code'' series by economist Scott Cunningham on the
\emph{Scott's Mixtape} Substack.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Over the past year, large language models moved from chat-style assistants to
\emph{agents}: systems that can read a codebase, plan a task, run commands,
inspect their own output, and iterate toward a goal with limited human
intervention. In research, this shift matters because the day-to-day labor of
empirical work---writing and debugging code, cleaning data, running estimators,
producing tables and figures, and drafting exposition---is precisely the kind
of multi-step, tool-using work at which agents now excel. The central question
is no longer \emph{whether} an agent can produce a piece of research, but
\emph{which stages} of research we should let it run, and under what safeguards.

\section{A Framework: Three Doors of Research}
A useful organizing idea, articulated in Cunningham's recent writing, is that a
research manuscript must pass through three distinct ``doors'':
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
  \item \textbf{Production} --- generating the artifact itself: the code, the
        data pipeline, the estimates, the tables, figures, and prose.
  \item \textbf{Verification} --- establishing that the artifact is
        \emph{correct}: that the pipeline runs, that the numbers are right, and
        that the claims follow from the analysis (the ``zero-errors'' constraint).
  \item \textbf{Understanding} --- a human genuinely comprehending what was done
        and why, so the work can be defended, extended, and trusted.
\end{enumerate}
The key observation is that these three were historically \emph{bundled}: when a
researcher produced work by hand, they necessarily verified and understood it
along the way. Agents \emph{unbundle} them. An agent can now handle production
end-to-end while verification and understanding become separate, deliberate
tasks that humans must not skip. Deciding how far to open each door---how much
autonomy to grant at production, and how much scrutiny to keep at verification
and understanding---is the core governance problem of AI-assisted research.

\section{What Claude Code Is}
Claude Code is a command-line agentic coding tool from Anthropic. Rather than
returning a snippet to copy and paste, it operates directly in a project:
reading files, editing them, running shell commands and tests, and reacting to
the results in a loop. For researchers this is significant because empirical
projects are effectively software projects---directories of scripts, data,
logs, and outputs---so an agent that can act on the whole project (not just a
single prompt) can carry a task from raw data to finished exhibit. Common
access patterns include a terminal on a workstation, an IDE integration, and
lightweight remote or mobile control for launching and checking on runs away
from the desk.

\section{Applications in Empirical Research}

\subsection{Reproducibility and coding audits}
An agent can be pointed at an existing analysis and asked to replicate a
published pipeline \emph{from scratch}, independently, as a check on the
original. A striking version of this is cross-implementation auditing:
re-deriving the same estimate---for example a difference-in-differences
result---across multiple languages (Python, R, Stata) and multiple packages,
and confirming the numbers agree. Convergence across independent
implementations is strong evidence of correctness; divergence localizes a bug.

\subsection{Pipeline automation and estimation ``harnesses''}
Much empirical labor is repetitive scaffolding: importing and cleaning data,
constructing variables, running a family of specifications, and assembling
output. Agents automate this, and the practice is maturing from ad-hoc prompts
toward reusable \emph{harnesses}---structured checklists and templates that
encode a correct workflow (e.g., a difference-in-differences harness) and that
are gradually hardening into software. The harness both speeds production and
standardizes verification, because the same checks run every time.

\subsection{Autonomous empirical paper generation}
At the frontier, agents choose and execute empirical projects with minimal
step-by-step direction. In one documented effort, an autonomous
program-evaluation project generated on the order of a thousand automated
empirical papers: humans controlled the \emph{prompting} and the guardrails,
while agents controlled the \emph{production}. This illustrates both the scale
now achievable and why verification and understanding must be handled
explicitly---volume does not substitute for correctness.

\subsection{Communication and presentation}
Beyond analysis, agents assist with the ``last mile'' of research:
building slide decks, formatting tables and figures, and drafting explanatory
text so that finished work can be presented and taught. This lowers the cost of
turning a correct result into something an audience can absorb.

\subsection{Remote and mobile workflows}
Because an agent's work is largely asynchronous---launch a task, let it run,
review the result---researchers increasingly dispatch and monitor runs
remotely, including from a phone, revisiting long-running analyses without being
tied to a workstation.

\section{Verification and the Zero-Error Constraint}
The hardest and least automatable door is verification. Empirical claims carry a
``zero-errors'' expectation: a single wrong number can invalidate a result.
Practical strategies to earn that confidence include (i) independent
re-implementation and cross-checking, as in multi-language audits; (ii)
automated tests and assertions baked into the harness; (iii) adversarial review,
where a second agent or a human is tasked specifically with trying to
\emph{refute} a result rather than confirm it; and (iv) keeping a human in the
loop at the points where an error would be most costly. Verification is where
human judgment retains the clearest comparative advantage.

\section{Human Understanding and Its Risks}
The subtler risk is to the third door. When production is delegated, the
researcher can end up with a correct result they do not fully understand---a
gap that undermines their ability to defend it in review, teach it, or build on
it. Sustaining understanding is an active choice: reading what the agent
produced, asking it to explain its reasoning, reconstructing key steps by hand,
and treating the agent as a collaborator to interrogate rather than an oracle to
trust. The goal is leverage \emph{with} comprehension, not comprehension traded
away for speed.

\section{Best Practices}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Decide autonomy per door.} Grant more autonomy at production;
        keep tight human control at verification and understanding.
  \item \textbf{Codify workflows as harnesses.} Turn repeated tasks into
        checklists and, eventually, software, so quality is reproducible.
  \item \textbf{Verify independently.} Prefer cross-implementation and
        adversarial checks over self-confirmation by the same agent.
  \item \textbf{Keep provenance.} Log prompts, code, data versions, and outputs
        so a result can be traced and reproduced.
  \item \textbf{Protect understanding.} Budget time to read, question, and
        internalize what the agent did before relying on it.
  \item \textbf{Scale prompting, not trust.} As output volume grows (hundreds of
        analyses), invest correspondingly in guardrails and review.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations and Open Questions}
Agentic research remains bounded by several open problems: the reliability of
verification at scale; the temptation to accept unverified output because it
``looks right''; reproducibility and provenance across many automated runs;
appropriate norms of disclosure and authorship when agents do much of the
production; and the pedagogical question of how students build understanding in
a world where production is cheap. These are governance and methodology
questions as much as technical ones, and they are the subject of active debate.

\section{Conclusion}
The practical value of AI in research is best understood not as ``the agent
writes the paper'' but as a deliberate reallocation of effort. Agents
increasingly own \emph{production}; humans must consciously retain
\emph{verification} and \emph{understanding}. Tools like Claude Code make the
first door cheap; the discipline of the researcher is what keeps the other two
open. Used this way, agentic tools compress the mechanical cost of empirical
work while preserving the correctness and comprehension on which credible
research depends.
