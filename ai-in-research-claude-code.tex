\begin{abstract}
\noindent This document summarizes the main concepts emerging around the
application of AI agents to research, with a specific emphasis on
\emph{Claude Code} as a tool for applied empirical and causal-inference
work. The synthesis draws on a run of 2026 essays by economist Scott
Cunningham, who has been documenting his practical experiments in using
AI agents to produce, verify, and understand quantitative research. The
organizing idea throughout is a triangle of \textbf{production},
\textbf{verification}, and \textbf{understanding}, held together by a
\emph{zero-errors constraint} and operationalized through checklists,
hooks, skills, connectors, and a bespoke ``harness'' for causal inference.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction: from ``3-D printing'' papers to a harness}

By early 2026 it was already both original and true to say that AI agents
could autonomously produce, start to finish, a manuscript of contemporary
quantitative empirical research. Prompted appropriately, tools such as
Claude Code or Codex could choose their own research question and
identification strategy, locate and download datasets, select estimators,
write replicable code, generate tables and figures with a battery of
robustness checks, and draft a full 20-page manuscript with references and
appendices---in roughly thirty minutes. Cunningham calls this
``3-D printing a research paper.''

The central lesson of the following year, however, is that end-to-end
\emph{production} is not the same as trustworthy research. The agent is
less a fully self-driving car than a vehicle with lane-keeping and cruise
control: capable, but requiring a human in the loop. The practical
research program, therefore, is not ``can the agent do it?'' (it can) but
``which stages should we let the agent drive, and how do we retain
accuracy and understanding while it does?''

\section{The core framework: production, verification, understanding}

The recurring conceptual backbone is a triangle. Any piece of research,
conditional on taste, must pass through three distinct ``doors'':

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item[Production.] Generating the artifact: the question, the code, the
  exhibits, the manuscript. This is the door AI agents already open with
  ease.
  \item[Verification.] Confirming the artifact is accurate---that what was
  \emph{supposed} to be done was in fact done, \emph{and nothing else}.
  \item[Understanding.] Human comprehension of what was done, why, and
  what it means. This is epistemic ownership, and it cannot be delegated.
\end{description}

Before AI agents, the act of production quietly \emph{bundled} the other
two: you could rarely write the code without also verifying and
understanding it. Agents unbundle them. Production can now happen without
either verification or understanding automatically following, so the
researcher must deliberately re-supply the missing two.

\subsection{Zero errors is the \emph{constraint}, not the objective}

A guiding principle is that \textbf{zero errors is a constraint}, not the
objective function. If error minimization becomes the goal, one implicitly
accepts an ``optimal'' positive level of errors. Instead, zero errors
enters as a hard constraint in the Lagrangean of producing a manuscript;
the objective function is optimal research \emph{output}---the point where
the social marginal benefit of research equals its social marginal
cost---subject to that constraint, which in turn requires production,
verification, and understanding.

\subsection{Why agents should not verify alone}

Agents are not merely fallible verifiers; they are structurally
insufficient ones. Two reasons stand out:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item Verification is more than re-running code in multiple languages or
  confirming that every figure traces to stored code. Those are
  \emph{necessary} but not \emph{sufficient} conditions.
  \item Verification is endogenous to an \emph{ex ante} target estimand: it
  must confirm both that the intended analysis was done and that
  unintended analyses were \emph{not}. An agent cannot verify the absence
  of something it never generated. Cunningham reports observing
  ``endogenous sorting'' into specifications---agents drifting toward
  particular model choices in response to true statements in a project's
  context, not just explicit instructions.
\end{itemize}
The researcher is cast as the \textbf{CEO} of the research enterprise:
delegation is fine, but the CEO must still know, in an approximately
accurate sense, what was done, by whom, and what was found.

\section{Understanding is the new bottleneck}

A popular slogan holds that ``\emph{verification} is the new bottleneck''
in an age of cheap generation. Cunningham refines this---following
Geoffrey Litt's essay ``Understanding is the new bottleneck'' and Paul
Goldsmith-Pinkham's framing---to argue that \textbf{understanding} is the
true binding constraint. Agents may eventually verify as well as they
produce, but verification does not guarantee that the human
\emph{understands} the result.

\subsection{An economic sketch of the shift}

The argument is framed with a simple supply-and-demand intuition for
research output at the individual level:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Before AI}: research is produced up to the point where its
  marginal benefit equals a relatively high marginal cost $MC_b$, fixed by
  scarce time and human capital.
  \item \textbf{With AI agents}: the marginal cost collapses toward a small
  $MC_{AI}\approx\varepsilon$, so quantity of research rises and new
  surplus is created---both on the old work (now cheaper) and on newly
  feasible work.
\end{itemize}
The subtle problem is the \emph{nature} of the old surplus. Historically,
understanding was almost a \emph{byproduct}---an externality---of the act
of production, much as pollution is a byproduct of manufacturing. If
understanding was always produced ``by accident'' while doing the work,
then removing the work may remove the understanding, and it is not obvious
that the time freed up can be reallocated to reconstruct it.

\subsection{Cognitive debt and the principal--agent risk}

Goldsmith-Pinkham proposes that the \emph{unit of verification} is the
\texttt{git diff}: review each change as it is proposed. Cunningham adopts
this but warns of a failure mode---skimming and silently accepting diffs
accrues ``\textbf{cognitive debt}'' rather than clearing it. Left
unchecked, the researcher becomes a principal who trusts an agent that
cannot be held accountable, risking ``the blind leading the blind'' as
human skills atrophy. He invokes the IBM maxim: a machine cannot be held
accountable, so it cannot be permitted to make a decision---agents must
either help us understand their work, or not be used.

\section{Claude Code in practice: checklists, hooks, skills}

The response to these risks is a deliberate \emph{workflow} that forces
research back through verification and understanding.

\subsection{The checklist philosophy}

Borrowing from Atul Gawande's \emph{The Checklist Manifesto} and Daniel
Kahneman's System~1 / System~2 distinction, the workflow is anchored in
explicit checklists. The motivating observation is that
\emph{experts}---not beginners---most often skip critical steps, because
expertise runs on fast, intuitive System~1 thinking. Checklists re-impose
System~2 discipline and are the vehicle for routing each project through
production, verification, and understanding. The lineage is traced to
design-first thinking in causal inference (Rubin's ``Design Trumps
Analysis''; the practical checklists in Guido Imbens's work).

\subsection{Hooks, skills, and a \texttt{/quiz} for understanding}

Concrete Claude Code mechanisms include:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item[Hooks.] Automated guardrails wired to specific stages---e.g., a
  hard rule to never edit raw data, or hooks dedicated to figure/table
  production and to assembling exhibits into a deck or manuscript. The
  emphasis is on \emph{narrow}, stage-specific hooks rather than abstract,
  universal ones.
  \item[Skills.] Reusable, invokable procedures. An \texttt{/interview}
  skill elicits the researcher's own beliefs (e.g., which covariates to
  include, what the target parameter should be).
  \item[The \texttt{/quiz} skill.] To combat silent acceptance of diffs,
  Claude Code generates teaching slides and then quizzes the researcher
  with one-at-a-time multiple-choice questions---modeled on corporate
  ``training-video-plus-quiz'' compliance formats---to actively rebuild
  \emph{continuous understanding} on top of verification.
\end{description}

\subsection{The difference-in-differences harness}

Cunningham's central artifact is a \textbf{harness} for causal
inference---initially a checklist, evolving toward software---deliberately
scoped to difference-in-differences so it can be evaluated and perfected.
It is intentionally \emph{visual} (partly owing to his aphantasia), with
figures presented as scrollable ``flippable cards'' to aid memory across a
project. Two early stages of the checklist:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Packages.} A visual card recording exactly which packages
  and versions the project uses (reproducibility).
  \item \textbf{Bite.} An exhaustive, descriptive study of the treatment's
  \emph{first-stage} effect on the world---what the intervention actually
  moves first---documented across \emph{space} (heat maps) and \emph{time}
  (time-series aggregations), for treatment and comparison groups alike.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Why ``bite'' should stay human.} The bite stage is where the
project is conceived and where insight into the \textbf{treatment
assignment mechanism} is formed---human rationality / essential
heterogeneity (Heckman, Roy; Rubin's ``perfect doctor''), instruments
(compliers vs.\ always-/never-takers; LATE), randomization, and running
variables (regression discontinuity). Because a truthful account of the
assignment mechanism is what overcomes the specification pitfalls
LaLonde~(1986) exposed, Cunningham argues the bite stage is precisely the
part that should \emph{not} be fully automated: agents can draw the maps
and write the code, but the human must own the detective work.

\subsection{Connecting theory to calculation}

A methodological thread (from the most recent newsletter, below) is worth
carrying into any AI-assisted workflow: difference-in-differences is
simultaneously \emph{a calculation}---``four averages and three
subtractions''---and \emph{a research design}, i.e., a calculation plus an
assumption (parallel trends) under which a selection-bias term is zero.
Viewing parallel trends as a \textbf{debiasing} operation (in the spirit
of Heckman, Ichimura, and Todd, 1997) clarifies what an agent is really
doing when it imputes a counterfactual trend---and opens the door to
flexible machine-learning imputation when covariates are numerous, so long
as they are known ex ante.

\section{Connectors and the Model Context Protocol (MCP)}

Beyond the local machine, \textbf{connectors} extend Claude into cloud
services and onto the phone (via the Claude app), enabling use away from
the desktop. Connectors are built on the \textbf{Model Context Protocol
(MCP)}, an open standard that lets AI applications securely connect to
external data sources and tools to access real-time information and take
actions---the ``piping'' that makes connectors work.

Key observations:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item The connector directory functions like an ``app store,'' with
  roughly 4{,}000 connectors and growing (hundreds under a ``Code''
  category alone).
  \item \textbf{Community} connectors are user-created and only lightly
  vetted---``buyer beware''---versus first-party ones.
  \item Practical uses today skew toward productivity and small-business
  operations (Dropbox search, Stripe/PayPal, QuickBooks, Gusto), plausibly
  fueling the rise of AI-enabled ``solopreneurs.'' Their research payoff
  is still being explored; the author expects connectors eventually to
  feed a visual causal-inference harness rather than to \emph{be} it.
\end{itemize}
