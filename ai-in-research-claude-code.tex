\begin{abstract}
This note summarizes the main concepts behind using AI coding agents --- in
particular \emph{Claude Code} --- as collaborators in quantitative empirical
research. It synthesizes the recurring themes of Scott Cunningham's
\emph{Scott's Mixtape} Substack, where causal-inference research is carried out
alongside AI agents. The central argument is that agents have made research
\emph{production} nearly free, which relocates the scarce, valuable human work
to \emph{verification} and \emph{understanding}. Practical structure ---
checklists, harnesses, hooks, and skills --- is what keeps agent-assisted
research honest.
\end{abstract}

\section{The shift: production is (almost) free}
AI agents can now autonomously carry a contemporary empirical paper from
question to manuscript: propose a research question, pick an identification
strategy, locate and download datasets, choose estimators, write replicable
code, generate tables and figures with robustness checks, and draft a
20-page manuscript with references and appendices --- often in under an hour.
Cunningham calls this ``3-d printing a research paper.''

The key economic observation is that the \emph{marginal cost of producing}
research output has collapsed. But cheap production is not the same as
trustworthy knowledge. The bottleneck moves downstream --- from making the
artifact to being warranted in believing it.

\section{The research triangle: production, verification, understanding}
A useful mental model is that agent-assisted research must pass through three
distinct ``doors'':
\begin{description}[style=nextline, leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item[Production] Generating the analysis and the manuscript. Agents do this
    well and can do it autonomously and endogenously (choosing the topic and
    design themselves).
  \item[Verification] Confirming the result is accurate \emph{with respect to a
    pre-stated target estimand}. This is more than re-running code in several
    languages or checking that every exhibit is script-generated --- those are
    necessary but not sufficient. Verification must also confirm that what
    \emph{should not} have happened did not happen (e.g., no endogenous drift
    into a favorable specification). Agents cannot fully self-verify this: there
    is nothing positive to check when the task is to confirm an \emph{absence}.
  \item[Understanding] The human must retain full, accurate understanding of
    what was done, by whom, and what was found. The analogy is a CEO who
    delegates but still knows, approximately and correctly, the state of every
    factor of production.
\end{description}

\noindent A guiding constraint is \textbf{zero errors} --- treated as a hard
constraint rather than an objective to be ``minimized'' (which would license
an ``optimal'' positive error rate). Collapsing the whole project into a single
manuscript is a convenient sufficient statistic: the manuscript must pass
through production, verification, and understanding.

\section{Structure over vibes: checklists and harnesses}
Because experts skip steps --- often more than beginners, a ``thinking fast''
(System~1) failure mode --- agent-assisted research benefits from explicit
structure. Cunningham is building a \emph{difference-in-differences harness}:
a narrow, checklist-driven, increasingly software-shaped workflow that centers
the research triangle. The narrowness is deliberate: a tightly scoped harness
(one method) is easier to evaluate and perfect than a universal one, though the
same idea extends to synthetic control, unconfoundedness, and other designs.

\subsection*{Illustrative checklist stages}
\begin{description}[style=nextline, leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item[Packages] A visual card recording which packages and \emph{which
    versions} the project uses --- reproducibility and defense against silent
    deprecation.
  \item[Bite] The first-stage question: when the intervention lands, what is
    the very first thing it moves? (For a gun law, track the movement of guns,
    not deaths; for a minimum wage, find where it binds.) Document ``bite''
    exhaustively across \emph{space} (heat maps) and \emph{time} (aggregated
    series), for treatment and comparison groups alike.
\end{description}

\noindent The ``bite'' stage is where the treatment-assignment mechanism is
diagnosed --- the design-based core of credible causal inference. Candidate
mechanisms include human rationality / essential heterogeneity (endogenous
choice), instruments (randomized assignment of a nudge), direct randomization
(RCTs), and running variables (regression discontinuity).

\section{What to automate --- and what not to}
A central practical judgment: \emph{do not fully automate the stage where the
project is conceived.} Agents should build the maps, write the code, produce
the time-series figures, and wire exhibits into a deck or manuscript --- all
the mechanical production that supports verification. But the human should own
the ``bite'' / conception stage, where insight into the assignment mechanism
drives the choice of design and comparison group. Automate production; keep
verification and understanding human-supervised.

\section{Claude Code as a research instrument}
The concrete tooling that makes this workflow real:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Agentic end-to-end execution.} Claude Code operates on a local
    machine: reading and writing files, running code, downloading data, and
    iterating --- an ``autonomous-driving'' capability that is nonetheless
    better treated as advanced lane-assist than full self-driving.
  \item \textbf{Hooks.} Deterministic automations wired to specific checklist
    steps (e.g., regenerate figures/tables and re-embed them in the manuscript
    on change), rather than abstract, universal hooks.
  \item \textbf{Skills.} Packaged, reusable procedures for recurring craft
    tasks (figure and table production, specification templates).
  \item \textbf{MCP integrations.} Connecting the agent to external systems
    (data sources, publishing platforms, repositories) through the Model
    Context Protocol.
  \item \textbf{Reproducibility discipline.} Version-pinned environments and
    script-generated exhibits so that ``what the code says'' and ``what the
    paper shows'' cannot silently diverge.
\end{itemize}

\section{Open questions}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Model heterogeneity.} Do frontier models differ systematically
    in autonomous-research skill, and is that difference knowable \emph{ex
    ante}?
  \item \textbf{Endogenous specification search.} Agents can drift into
    favorable specifications in response to true statements about a project ---
    how do we detect and prevent it?
  \item \textbf{Generalizability of the harness.} How much of a method-specific
    harness transfers to other designs and other researchers' workflows?
\end{itemize}

\section*{Takeaways}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item Agents made \emph{production} cheap; the scarce goods are now
    \emph{verification} and \emph{understanding}.
  \item Verification is relative to a pre-committed estimand and must also rule
    out what should not have happened --- something agents cannot fully do
    alone.
  \item Human understanding is non-negotiable; the researcher is the CEO of the
    project, accountable under a zero-error constraint.
  \item Checklists, harnesses, hooks, and skills convert a powerful but
    over-eager agent into a disciplined research collaborator.
  \item Automate the mechanical production; keep conception, design choice, and
    final judgment in human hands.
\end{enumerate}
