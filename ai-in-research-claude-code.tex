\begin{abstract}
\noindent This document summarizes the main concepts surrounding the use of
artificial intelligence (AI) agents---particularly \keyword{Claude Code}---in
academic and applied research. Drawing on the reflections of economist Scott
Cunningham, it distills how AI coding agents are reshaping the practice of
empirical research, the problems they introduce, and the emerging workflows,
``harnesses,'' and disciplines that researchers are developing in response. The
running theme is that AI raises productivity dramatically while also creating a
new class of reproducibility and quality-control challenges that demand
deliberate, structured processes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Why AI Agents Matter for Research}

AI coding agents such as Claude Code allow a researcher to conduct data analysis
conversationally: describing a task in natural language and having the agent
write, execute, and revise code. This changes the economics of empirical work.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \keyword{Falling marginal cost of production.} Once a workflow is set
        up, the cost of producing an additional table, figure, robustness check,
        or falsification test falls sharply. Researchers can explore far more of
        the analytical space than was previously feasible.
  \item \keyword{Shift from writing code to specifying intent.} The researcher's
        comparative advantage moves toward defining the causal question,
        selecting estimators, and judging output---rather than typing syntax.
  \item \keyword{Volume and abundance.} A lengthy empirical study now generates
        an \emph{abundance} of files, figures, tables, and code---often far more
        than a human would have created by hand.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Core Problem: AI Agent Drift}

The central risk Cunningham identifies is what he calls \keyword{AI agent drift}:
the tendency of an agent, over a long project, to silently change the analysis in
ways the researcher does not notice.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \keyword{Silent sample changes.} An analytical sample can drift from $N$
        to $N+k$ observations with no explanation, corrupting inter-temporal
        comparisons even when contemporaneous results look correct.
  \item \keyword{Code proliferation.} What feels like editing one program can
        quietly become thousands of near-duplicate programs with confusing,
        similar-sounding names---a return of the old ``copies of the same code''
        problem, now at machine speed.
  \item \keyword{Distance from the work.} Automation can put the researcher at a
        greater remove from the underlying analysis, making errors harder to
        catch precisely because the surface output looks polished.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The lesson: productivity gains are real, but without controls they come
bundled with a heightened risk of untracked, unintended change.

\section{The Response: A Harness and a Rigid Checklist}

Cunningham's solution is to impose a \keyword{structured workflow}---a
``harness''---around the agent. He hypothesizes that a general
\emph{harness-workflow-for-empirical-research-using-AI-agents} will eventually be
articulated and widely adopted, while individual researchers still customize
their own dashboards by comparative advantage.

His own checklist runs to roughly 12--14 rigid stages for a causal-inference
project:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.8em, label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
  \item \keyword{PKG} --- enumerate all software packages used.
  \item \keyword{TARGET} --- define the causal parameter(s) in potential-outcomes
        notation, with populations and weights.
  \item \keyword{BITE} --- document the treatment's ``bite'' with 2--3 exhibits
        illustrating the intervention in the world.
  \item \keyword{COVARIATES} --- select covariates for unconfoundedness,
        conditional parallel trends, or a factor model; assess balance.
  \item \keyword{ROLLOUT} --- document the treatment rollout by cohort
        (e.g.\ via Xu's \texttt{panelview}).
  \item \keyword{OUTCOME} --- present the outcome, matched to the intended analysis.
  \item \keyword{POWER} --- run power calculations, minimum detectable effects,
        and over-rejection checks.
  \item \keyword{ESTIMATOR} --- choose and estimate the estimator, flowing from
        TARGET, COVARIATES, and BITE.
  \item \keyword{EVENT} --- produce event-study graphics.
  \item \keyword{FALSIFY} --- run falsification tests (placebo outcomes and groups).
  \item \keyword{MECHANISM} --- investigate candidate mechanisms.
  \item \keyword{PIPELINE} --- re-run everything to confirm replication.
  \item \keyword{DELIVER} --- storyboard and draft deliverables.
  \item \keyword{SIGN} --- sign off.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The checklist is simultaneously a statement of \emph{design principles}
and a direct \emph{reaction to agent drift}. It draws on established
methodological guidance: Rubin's ``Design Trumps Analysis,'' difference-in-differences
checklists (e.g.\ Sant'Anna), and Imbens's survey recommendations.

\section{Tooling: Dashboards, Skills, and Hooks}

Around the checklist, Cunningham builds concrete tooling to enforce discipline:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \keyword{A visual dashboard} with color-coded status circles---green
        (closed / verified), red (open), and a pulsing amber (``you are here'').
        No project can close while any circle is red.
  \item \keyword{Custom skills} such as \texttt{/covariates} (candidate-covariate
        selection from a stated modeling philosophy), \texttt{/referee2}, and
        \texttt{/blindspot}, run repeatedly as compulsive double-checks.
  \item \keyword{Rituals per stage}---a fixed set of steps that must be completed
        before a stage's circle turns green.
  \item \keyword{Hooks, locks, and maps}---mechanisms that let both the
        researcher's future self and ``future Claudes'' pick up exactly where
        work left off.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Canon Metaphor: Curating a Replicable Pipeline}

Cunningham's most striking framing likens the researcher's task to how the early
church settled its \keyword{canon}. Faced with many overlapping letters and
books---similar names, similar but non-identical stories---a process of
\emph{selection and curation} decided what belonged and what was
\keyword{deprecated} (labeled ``apocryphal'' or ``heresy'').

\begin{quote}
The canon is the pipeline of code that produces the exhibits (tables and figures)
supporting the final manuscript the author presents as their own.
\end{quote}

AI agents produce an analogous flood of near-duplicate artifacts. Researchers
therefore need an \keyword{explicit criterion, process, and rule} for canonizing
code into a replicable pipeline and deprecating the rest. Before AI, much of this
curation happened unconsciously; established guidance on file naming, directory
structure, and version control (e.g.\ Gentzkow and Shapiro's manual) foreshadows
it, but the AI-era version will be more deliberate and, at first, unfamiliar.

\section{Key Takeaways}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.8em]
  \item AI agents like Claude Code sharply raise research productivity by lowering
        the marginal cost of analysis.
  \item Their main hazard is \keyword{drift}: silent, untracked changes to samples,
        code, and results over long projects.
  \item The remedy is a deliberate \keyword{harness}: rigid, staged checklists that
        encode both methodological design and error control.
  \item \keyword{Tooling}---dashboards, custom skills, hooks, and status locks---operationalizes
        the checklist and preserves continuity across sessions and agents.
  \item Researchers must consciously \keyword{curate a canon}, choosing which code
        and exhibits enter the replicable pipeline and deprecating the rest, so
        that results provably mean what the author claims.
  \item A shared, general workflow standard for AI-assisted empirical research is
        likely to emerge, even as individuals tailor their own dashboards.
\end{enumerate}
