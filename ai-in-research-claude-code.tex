\begin{abstract}
\noindent This document distills the main concepts that have emerged in Scott
Cunningham's ``Claude Code'' essays on using AI agents for research. It is
organized around a single governing idea: that empirical research produced with
AI agents must pass through three stages---\emph{production}, \emph{verification},
and \emph{understanding}---under a \emph{zero-error constraint}. Around that spine
it collects the economics of research under agents (``understanding is the new
bottleneck''), the boundaries of autonomy, the checklist/harness philosophy, the
governance of agents through markdowns, skills, and hooks, and the emerging role
of connectors and the Model Context Protocol (MCP). It is a conceptual map, not
an endorsement of any single tool, and all interpretive framing is attributed to
the source essays.
\end{abstract}

\section{Context and Motivation}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

AI coding agents---Claude Code chief among them in these essays---have made it
possible to run a full quantitative empirical project largely from natural-language
instruction: forming a question, choosing an identification strategy, locating and
downloading data, writing replicable code, producing tables and figures, and
drafting a manuscript. The author reports that, as early as six months prior,
an agent could ``3-D print'' a submittable paper end to end in well under an hour.

The interesting problem is therefore \emph{not} whether agents can produce research.
They can. The problem is that raw productive capability does not by itself deliver
\emph{trustworthy} or \emph{understood} research. The essays are an extended attempt
to name what is missing and to engineer it back in.

\section{The Central Framework: Production, Verification, Understanding}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The organizing metaphor is a triangle. Research done with agents must pass through
three distinct ``doors'':

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2em,style=nextline]
  \item[\term{Production}] Generating the analysis and artifacts: code, datasets,
        exhibits, and prose. This is the stage agents already handle well and can
        do autonomously.
  \item[\term{Verification}] Confirming the output is accurate---\emph{and} that
        what should \emph{not} have been done was not done. Verification is
        endogenous to a \emph{target estimand fixed ex ante}; it is not merely
        re-running code in several languages. Agents can assist, but should not be
        relied on exclusively, because there is a class of errors (things that
        should not have happened) that leave nothing for an agent to check.
  \item[\term{Understanding}] Genuine human comprehension of what was done and why.
        This is framed as the scarcest of the three and the hardest to recover once
        the human is no longer the producer.
\end{description}

The author's own shorthand for the goal is simply ``production, verification, and
understanding.''

\subsection{The CEO metaphor}
The researcher is cast as the \emph{CEO} of the research enterprise. A CEO
delegates---she need not write the code---but she must still hold an approximately
correct understanding of what was done, by whom, and what was found. Accountability
does not transfer to a delegate, and (invoking the old IBM line) a machine cannot
be held accountable, so it cannot be handed the final decision.

\section{The Economics of Research Under AI Agents}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Cunningham frames the shift with a supply-and-demand picture that will be intuitive
to economists:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item Before agents, research was produced up to the point where marginal benefit
        equalled a relatively high marginal cost $MC_b$, itself fixed by scarce
        time and human capital.
  \item With agents, the marginal cost of production collapses toward
        $\widehat{MC}_{AI}\approx \varepsilon$ (just above zero), so equilibrium
        research output rises from $Q^{*}_b$ to $Q^{*}_{AI}$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation*}
  MC_b \;\longrightarrow\; \widehat{MC}_{AI} \approx \varepsilon
  \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
  Q^{*}_b \;\longrightarrow\; Q^{*}_{AI}, \quad Q^{*}_{AI} > Q^{*}_b .
\end{equation*}

\subsection{Understanding as a lost byproduct}
The subtle point is about \emph{surplus}. Under the human mode of production,
understanding was generated \emph{almost as a byproduct} of doing the work---an
externality of production, ``mental ownership'' acquired by making the thing.
When the agent produces instead, that byproduct is no longer automatic. The freed-up
time \emph{could} in principle be reallocated to rebuild understanding, but there is
no guarantee that understanding produced deliberately can fully replace understanding
that used to arrive for free.

\subsection{``Understanding is the new bottleneck''}
A popular slogan holds that \emph{verification} is the new bottleneck. The essays
push further: because agents may eventually verify as well as they produce, the
truly binding constraint is \term{understanding}, not verification. Two related
cautions:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \term{Cognitive debt.} Treating the \texttt{git diff} as ``the unit of
        verification'' (an idea credited to Paul Goldsmith-Pinkham) helps, but
        skimming and silently accepting diffs accrues cognitive debt rather than
        clearing it.
  \item \term{Principal--agent risk.} Trusting an agent you cannot check is a
        principal--agent problem; if human skills atrophy, it becomes ``the blind
        leading the blind.''
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Zero errors as a constraint, not an objective}
A recurring formal stance: \emph{zero errors is a constraint, not the objective
function}. Minimizing errors quietly licenses the notion of ``optimal errors,''
which is unacceptable. Instead, optimal research output is pursued
\emph{subject to} a zero-error constraint that itself requires verification and
understanding:
\begin{equation*}
  \max_{\text{research}} \; (\text{research output})
  \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \text{errors} = 0,
\end{equation*}
where satisfying the constraint is what demands verification and understanding.

\section{The Boundaries of Autonomy}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

A guiding analogy: today's agents are less like a fully self-driving car and more
like \emph{lane-keeping plus cruise control}---impressive assistance, but not
unsupervised autonomy. Evidence that autonomous \emph{production} is real includes
large-scale demonstrations (e.g., a lab generating on the order of a thousand
automated program-evaluation papers, with humans controlling the prompting and
agents controlling the production).

The practical stance that follows:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \term{Automate freely} the mechanical parts of production: writing code,
        downloading and organizing data, making maps, time-series aggregations,
        tables, and figures, and wiring exhibits into a deck or manuscript.
  \item \term{Do not fully automate conception.} The stage where the project is
        understood and framed should stay human-led (see the ``bite'' stage below).
  \item \term{Watch for endogenous specification search.} Agents have been observed
        sorting into specifications not because they were told to, but in response to
        true statements made in a project's context---a reason verification must be
        anchored to a pre-registered estimand.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Harness and the Checklist Philosophy}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The proposed answer to the ``something is not quite right'' feeling is a
\term{harness}: a disciplined, visual staging of the whole workflow, built around a
\term{checklist}. The checklist idea is borrowed from Atul Gawande's
\emph{The Checklist Manifesto}: experts skip steps more than novices (Kahneman's
System~1 vs.\ System~2), so high-stakes work needs enforced steps. The harness is
described less as a tool than as ``a staging of my own cognition''---and is being
prototyped visually, including toward an Apple Vision Pro interface, partly to
compensate for the loss of ``seeing'' one's code that comes with delegating to agents
(the author has aphantasia and relies on visual, ``flippable card'' representations
to remember prior work).

\subsection{Design-based thinking and the ``bite'' stage}
The harness is explicitly rooted in \emph{design-based} causal inference (crediting
Rubin's ``Design Trumps Analysis,'' and the work of Imbens, Angrist, and
Sant'Anna). The first substantive checklist stage is \term{bite}: exhaustively
documenting the first-stage, boots-on-the-ground response to a treatment before any
estimation---mapping it across \emph{space} and \emph{time}. Bite is where the
\emph{treatment assignment mechanism} is deciphered, which in turn governs the credible
design:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \term{Human rationality / essential heterogeneity} (endogenous selection)
        makes naive causal inference hard.
  \item \term{Instruments} assign treatment to compliers $\Rightarrow$ instrumental
        variables (with the exclusion restriction).
  \item \term{Randomization} on the treatment itself $\Rightarrow$ RCT logic.
  \item \term{Running variables} (e.g., a legal threshold) $\Rightarrow$ regression
        discontinuity.
\end{itemize}
The point of emphasis: \emph{bite} is the least automatable stage, because it is
where insight and understanding are formed, even though agents can execute all of its
mechanical outputs.

\subsection{Interviews and the \texttt{/quiz} skill}
To manufacture understanding deliberately, the author experiments with agent-run
``interviews'' that elicit the researcher's own priors (e.g., which covariates,
which target parameter), and a \texttt{/quiz} skill in which the agent builds
teaching slides and then asks the human multiple-choice questions about them---modeled
on corporate ``training video + quiz'' formats---to force \emph{continuous
understanding} on top of verification.

\section{Governing Agents: Markdowns, Skills, and Hooks}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

A central practical lesson: \emph{prose does not constrain an agent}. Markdown
instructions (\texttt{CLAUDE.md}, README, prompts) produced both false positives
and false negatives. The author organizes the three control mechanisms in a
$3\times 3$ matrix (who runs it, whether it can be skipped, when it is chosen),
and maps them to the branches of government:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2em,style=nextline]
  \item[\term{Markdowns} (legislative)] State the rules, but assume the agent will
        read and follow them. Necessary context; not enforcement.
  \item[\term{Skills} (e.g.\ \texttt{/referee2}, \texttt{/beautiful\_deck})] Reusable
        procedures, but assume the \emph{human} remembers to invoke them.
  \item[\term{Hooks} (executive)] Scripts that intercept the agent's tool calls and
        actually \emph{permit or deny} them. Hooks are the only genuinely enforceable
        layer.
\end{description}

\subsection{Drift}
\term{Drift} is the author's name for an agent quietly doing its own thing despite the
markdown rules---not malice, but lapsed judgment or cost-minimizing shortcuts. A
vivid example: an agent ``vibe-coded'' a synthetic-control permutation inference
directly in the terminal, producing a figure but writing \emph{no reproducible
script}, so the result could not be edited or replicated afterward. Every caught
instance raises the haunting question of how many went uncaught. Drift also shows up
as proliferating new files and stale, un-rerunnable code.

\subsection{How a hook works}
Because an agent ``has no hands,'' every action passes through a named tool
(\texttt{Read}, \texttt{Edit}, \texttt{Write}, \texttt{Bash}), each announced in
advance as a structured request. A hook stands at that announcement, inspects one or
two fields, and answers yes/no \emph{before} anything happens. Two mechanics:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \term{Pre-action (``locked door'')}: cancels a disallowed action before it
        runs---independent of any prose sign.
  \item \term{Post-action (``shout'')}: fires after the fact so a problem is fixed in
        the same sitting rather than days later.
  \item \term{Fails open}: if the hook's own code breaks, it lets the human through
        rather than freezing the workflow---a deliberate guard against
        over-engineering.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The canonical example: \texttt{protect-raw-data}}
The flagship hook enforces one axiom: \emph{raw data is untouchable}. It registers
only against \emph{mutating} tools (\texttt{Write}, \texttt{Edit}, \texttt{Bash});
if the target path lives under \texttt{/data/raw}, the call is denied with an
instruction to write to a derived folder (e.g.\ \texttt{/data/clean}) instead.
Crucially, \texttt{Read} is left open---the agent may look at every byte; it simply
cannot alter the original. This works where markdown failed because nothing must be
``held in mind'': the path either starts with \texttt{raw/} or it does not, and that
single question is asked on every write, forever. The stated set of five hooks:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,label=\arabic*.]
  \item \texttt{protect-raw-data}
  \item \texttt{no-fabricated-exhibit}
  \item \texttt{no-off-book-exhibit}
  \item \texttt{deck-from-pipeline}
  \item \texttt{no-stale-canon}
\end{enumerate}
The design rule behind them: \emph{anything that can happen will happen with enough
trials}, so fatal failure modes must be driven to probability zero by construction,
not merely discouraged.

\section{Connectors and the Model Context Protocol (MCP)}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The most recent essay turns to \term{connectors}: the mechanism that extends Claude
from a local machine into cloud services and onto the phone (the Claude app),
including for ``normal users'' who never touch Claude Code itself. Connectors are
powered by the \term{Model Context Protocol (MCP)}---an open standard that lets AI
applications connect securely to external data sources and tools and take actions;
in the essay's metaphor, ``a new kind of piping'' into cloud services.

Observations from the connector directory (thousands of connectors, hundreds tagged
``Code''):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \term{Two tiers}: Anthropic-originated connectors vs.\ \term{Community}
        (user-created, peer-reviewed but not fully vetted---``buyer beware'').
  \item \term{Productivity / assistant} connectors (e.g., an ``Inkbox''-style
        communication identity giving an assistant email/SMS/iMessage) could support
        a full personal-productivity system.
  \item \term{Financial-services} connectors (Stripe, PayPal, QuickBooks, Gusto,
        Dropbox) could let one person run a small business; notably \emph{banking}
        access is largely absent, plausibly for security reasons.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The ``solopreneur'' thesis and productivity}
Connectors are linked to the rise of \term{solo-entrepreneurs}---very small (often
one-person) firms, some reportedly reaching \$1M--\$10M in revenue---because they
make the chatbot side materially more productive. This is tied back to growth
economics: labor productivity ($Y/N$) drives per-capita income
($Y/\text{Pop}$), feeding both ``the wealth of nations'' (growth) and what the author
calls ``the wages of nations'' (distribution). For \emph{research} specifically, the
payoff of connectors is still uncertain; the author expects the eventual causal-inference
harness to make heavy use of them while remaining a visual, GUI-driven staging of
production, verification, and understanding.

\section{Human-in-the-Loop and the Learning Motive}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

A related essay (prompted by a Hugging Face / Data \& Society paper) asks \emph{where}
and \emph{how} the human should remain in the loop. The through-line across all the
essays is a preference for agents that \emph{sit in the cockpit} with the researcher
rather than autopilot while the researcher sleeps---motivated less by competition than
by a ``romance about learning.'' The technical mechanisms (checklists, hooks,
quizzes, visual harnesses) are all in service of keeping a human meaningfully
understanding the work.

\section{Practical Takeaways}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,label=\arabic*.]
  \item Treat every research artifact as needing to pass \term{production},
        \term{verification}, and \term{understanding}---not just production.
  \item Fix the \term{target estimand ex ante}; make verification confirm both what
        \emph{was} done and what \emph{should not} have been done.
  \item Automate mechanical production; keep \term{conception} (the ``bite'' stage)
        human-led.
  \item Do not rely on prose to constrain agents; encode non-negotiables as
        \term{hooks} that intercept tool calls, and let them \emph{fail open}.
  \item Protect raw data by construction (\texttt{protect-raw-data}); keep a numbered,
        rerunnable pipeline as the single source of every number, table, and figure.
  \item Budget deliberate time to rebuild the \term{understanding} that agent
        production no longer generates for free (e.g., interviews, a \texttt{/quiz}
        routine, visual review).
  \item Explore \term{connectors/MCP} to extend workflows into cloud services and the
        phone, distinguishing vetted from Community connectors.
  \item Hold \term{zero errors} as a constraint, not an objective.
\end{enumerate}
