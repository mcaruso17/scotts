\begin{abstract}
\noindent This document summarizes the main concepts surrounding the use of
artificial intelligence (AI) agents in academic and applied research, with a
particular focus on \concept{Claude Code} as a research assistant. The themes
are drawn from the ongoing ``Claude Code'' essay series in Scott Cunningham's
\emph{Scott's Mixtape} newsletter, which documents---in real time---how an
empirical economist is restructuring his research workflow around AI agents.
The aim is to distill the recurring ideas: what AI agents do well, what new
problems they introduce, and the emerging vocabulary (harnesses, skills,
context, drift) that researchers are developing to work with them reliably.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

AI coding agents such as Claude Code have moved from being autocomplete tools to
becoming collaborators in the full research pipeline---reading data, writing and
running analysis code, producing tables and figures, and managing version
control. For empirical researchers this is not merely a faster way of doing the
old work; it is a \concept{shift in the production function of research itself}.
The central message of Cunningham's series is that AI agents ``solve many
historic problems but also create new ones,'' and that the researcher's job is
to design the systems that capture the gains while controlling the new failure
modes.

\section{What AI Agents Do Well in Research}

Claude Code reproduces, automatically, many of the disciplined practices that
careful empirical researchers spent years building by hand:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \concept{Replicable code.} It writes pipelines rather than one-off
        scripts, supporting reproducibility.
  \item \concept{Organized project structure.} It maintains a sensible directory
        layout and consistent file/folder naming conventions.
  \item \concept{Automated exhibits.} Tables and figures are generated
        programmatically from the data and code, not assembled by hand.
  \item \concept{Version control.} Because git operations are accessible to the
        agent, commits and history tracking become routine.
  \item \concept{Lower mechanical error rate.} Many classes of clerical and
        coding errors that historically required vigilance are handled by the
        agent ``so much better than me.''
\end{itemize}

\noindent In short, the agent embodies, by default, the empirical hygiene that
researchers previously had to enforce on themselves through habit.

\section{The Core Vocabulary}

A new lexicon has emerged for working with AI agents. Three terms anchor the
discussion.

\subsection{Skills}
A \concept{skill} (e.g. \texttt{/beautiful\_deck} for slide decks or
\texttt{/referee2} for code validation) is a reusable, atomistic capability: do
the normal task, but invoke a skill to do it. Skills are powerful but
\concept{idiosyncratic to the individual}, and---crucially---they are
\emph{tools}, not a \emph{philosophy}. A pile of good skills does not by itself
constitute a working research system.

\subsection{The Harness}
An \concept{agent harness} is ``the complete architectural system surrounding an
LLM that manages the lifecycle of context: from intent capture through
specification, compilation, execution, verification, and persistence''---that
is, \emph{everything except the model itself}. The harness is what connects the
model to the outside world: tools, memory stores, and orchestrated workflows. In
the research context, building a harness means designing the personal
infrastructure---logs, dashboards, progress summaries---that makes the agent's
work legible and reliable over time. Cunningham frames this as ``building a house
from the ground up,'' where the harness is the physical expression of one's
research workflow.

\subsection{Context and Memory}
AI agents ``operate within an ecosystem of tools, memory stores, and
orchestrated workflows.'' Managing \concept{context}---what the agent knows,
remembers, and persists between sessions---is the central engineering problem.
Memory that is not deliberately persisted is effectively lost when a session
ends.

\section{New Problems: Drift and Amnesia}

The most important conceptual contribution of the series is naming the new
failure modes that AI-driven research introduces.

\subsection{Drift}
\concept{Drift} is the silent, gradual divergence of a project from its intended
state. Concrete examples include:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item A script believed to exist was never actually written---it was produced
        ad hoc during dialogue and never committed to disk.
  \item The \concept{analytical sample shifts over time}, implying a script is
        being modified unknowingly.
  \item \concept{Treatment units subtly change} from what they were months
        earlier.
\end{itemize}
The danger of drift is not that errors occur, but that they occur
\concept{outside the researcher's usual error-detection locations}, so they are
not caught in real time.

\subsection{The ``Claude Mess''}
Conventional human messes \emph{look} like messes and are therefore easy to
spot. An agent's output is a \concept{well-designed kind of sprawl}: many
scripts, many exhibits, some stale---``a Claude mess, not a human mess''---which
is precisely what makes it hard to recognize, diagnose, and fix. It is a modern
version of the old ``poor man's version control'' problem of keeping ten
date-stamped copies of a file.

\subsection{Amnesia and the Human-in-the-Loop}
When the researcher no longer types the code, the \concept{flow state} that once
imprinted a project into muscle memory disappears. Putting a project down for
even two days produces an ``unusually amnesiac'' return. The proposed remedies
are systemic rather than tool-based:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \concept{Progress logs} written by the agent after every major session.
  \item A daily-generated \concept{dashboard / ``beautiful deck''} that reads
        those logs and re-orients the researcher to the project.
  \item A small set of \concept{5--6 habitual policies} that make errors
        ``knowable and controllable,'' rather than another skill.
  \item An \concept{analog notebook} to map what the agent is doing and to
        constrain its latitude.
\end{itemize}
The underlying principle: \emph{build systems endogenous to how you actually
work, rather than systems that assume the problem away.}

\section{Workflow Philosophy}

A recurring theme is that \concept{tools are endogenous to a philosophy}, not the
other way around. Borrowing from David Allen's \emph{Getting Things Done},
Cunningham argues the right starting point is a productivity \emph{philosophy}
from which the specific tools (skills, harnesses, dashboards) are derived. The
key design question is positional: \concept{``Where do I insert myself in this
new production function?''}---i.e., how to design the optimal human-in-the-loop
system that simultaneously raises research productivity and drives errors toward
zero.

A related stylistic observation: Cunningham works \concept{conversationally},
co-authoring the research process in real-time dialogue rather than dictating
tasks or pre-designing multi-step orchestration in ``plan mode.'' This dialogic
style maximizes discovery but, by removing the researcher from the scaffolding,
appears to be a source of drift---underscoring that workflow style and error
profile are tightly linked.

\section{Adjacent Themes: Scientific Norms and Disclosure}

The series situates AI tools within broader questions of scientific practice,
including \concept{AI disclosure} in published research and the governance of
academic publishing. As AI agents become co-producers of empirical results, the
norms for \emph{how} their contribution is disclosed and verified become part of
the research-integrity conversation. The motivating standard throughout is a
demand for \concept{reproducibility and a near-zero error rate}, now pursued
through agent-assisted---rather than purely manual---means.

\section{Key Takeaways}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item AI agents reproduce empirical best practices (replicable pipelines,
        organized structure, automated exhibits, version control) by default.
  \item They shift the \emph{production function} of research; the new
        equilibrium must be deliberately designed, not assumed.
  \item \concept{Skills} are reusable tools; a \concept{harness} is the
        surrounding system that manages context, memory, and verification.
  \item The signature new risks are \concept{drift} (silent divergence) and
        \concept{amnesia} (loss of the researcher's internalized project memory).
  \item An agent's ``mess'' is dangerous because it looks organized---errors
        appear outside one's habitual detection zones.
  \item Robust solutions are \concept{systemic and habitual} (logs, dashboards,
        a few firm policies), built \concept{endogenously} to the researcher's
        own working style.
  \item The enduring goal is unchanged: \concept{reproducible research with
        errors driven to zero}, now achieved with the human positioned
        intentionally in the loop.
\end{enumerate}
