\begin{abstract}
\noindent This document summarizes the main concepts that are emerging around the
use of AI agents in scientific research, with particular attention to
\textbf{Claude Code} as a coding agent for empirical work. It draws on the
recent essays of economist Scott Cunningham (Baylor University; author of
\emph{Causal Inference: The Mixtape} and \emph{Causal Inference: The Remix}),
who has documented in real time how AI agents change the production,
verification, and understanding of quantitative research. The goal is a compact,
conceptual reference rather than a how-to manual.
\end{abstract}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\section{From Chatbots to Coding Agents}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

A useful first distinction separates three ``harnesses'' that wrap the same
underlying language models, because conflating them is the single most common
source of confusion:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Chatbots} (e.g.\ ChatGPT, the Claude app) --- conversational
        interfaces most people already know.
  \item \textbf{Coding copilots} (e.g.\ Cursor, GitHub Copilot) --- inline
        assistance embedded in an editor.
  \item \textbf{Coding agents} (e.g.\ Claude Code, Codex) --- agents that operate
        directly on your machine: reading and writing files across the hard
        drive, running shell commands, downloading data, executing code, and
        iterating toward a goal.
\end{itemize}

The critical conceptual point is that \emph{AI agents for research are not
chatbots}. A researcher's prior familiarity with ChatGPT can actively
\emph{impede} understanding of what an agent does, because it invites the false
belief that the work reduces to finding the ``right prompt.'' In practice,
serious users do not ``prompt-engineer'' an agent so much as \emph{converse}
with it as a thinking partner; the relevant skill is closer to collaboration
than to incantation.

\section{What Autonomous Empirical Research Can Now Do}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

A coding agent can, start to finish and largely unattended, carry out a complete
empirical study: propose a research question, choose an identification strategy,
locate and download public datasets, select an estimator, write replicable code,
produce publication-quality tables and figures, run batteries of robustness
checks, and draft a full manuscript with references and appendices --- often in
well under an hour. Cunningham calls this ``3-D printing a research paper.''
Large-scale demonstrations (e.g.\ autonomous program-evaluation projects that
generated on the order of a thousand papers using difference-in-differences,
instrumental variables, and related designs) have moved this from conjecture to
established fact.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{But full autonomy remains out of reach.} A helpful metaphor:
today's agents are less like a fully self-driving car and more like adaptive
cruise control with lane-keeping. They can \emph{produce} research
autonomously, yet they cannot be trusted to \emph{verify} their own accuracy or
to supply the researcher's \emph{understanding}. This gap motivates the central
framework below.

\section{The Core Framework: Production, Verification, Understanding}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The organizing idea is that research with agents must pass through three
distinct ``doors,'' conditional on the researcher's taste and judgment:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{Production} --- generating the research artifact (code, exhibits,
        the manuscript itself). This is the door agents already open well.
  \item \textbf{Verification} --- confirming that what was intended was in fact
        done, \emph{and} that what should not have been done was not done.
        Verification is defined relative to a pre-specified target estimand; it
        is not merely re-running code in several languages or confirming that a
        figure was produced by stored code. Those are \emph{necessary but not
        sufficient} conditions.
  \item \textbf{Understanding} --- the researcher's own warranted comprehension
        of the work. This is fundamentally a human obligation and cannot be
        delegated to the agent.
\end{enumerate}

A recurring cautionary observation: agents can exhibit \emph{endogenous sorting
into model specifications} not because they were instructed to, but in response
to true statements made within a project's context. This is precisely why
verification cannot be outsourced wholesale to the agent --- part of what must
be verified is the \emph{absence} of choices that were never supposed to be
made, and ``there is nothing to verify'' about an omission the agent silently
introduced.

\subsection{Zero Errors as a Constraint, Not an Objective}

A key normative stance: \textbf{``zero errors'' should be treated as a
\emph{constraint}, not as the objective function.} If error \emph{minimization}
becomes the goal, one implicitly accepts ``optimal errors'' as acceptable. The
research problem is better cast as maximizing (optimal) research output subject
to the hard constraint of zero errors --- a Lagrangean in which production,
verification, and understanding are all required to hold.

\subsection{The Researcher as CEO}

A guiding analogy: the researcher should act as the \textbf{CEO of the research
enterprise}. A CEO delegates --- she need not personally write every line of
code --- but she must nonetheless maintain an approximately accurate
understanding of \emph{what} was done, \emph{by whom}, and \emph{what was
found}. As delegation scales, so does the difficulty of retaining that
understanding, which is exactly the challenge agents create.

\section{The Economics of Research Production under AI}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Agents dramatically lower the \textbf{marginal cost of producing research},
from some pre-AI level $MC_b$ (fixed largely by human time and skill) to a level
$\widehat{MC}_{AI} \approx \varepsilon$, just above zero. Standard supply-and-demand
logic then implies:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item Research \textbf{quantity rises}, from $Q^*_b$ to $Q^*_{AI}$, with the
        magnitude governed by the elasticity of research demand.
  \item Existing work is now produced with far fewer time inputs, generating
        additional surplus; new work at the margin generates further surplus
        still.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{The subtle problem is what happens to ``understanding.''}
Historically, understanding was in large part a \emph{byproduct} of production
--- almost an externality of doing the work by hand (a kind of ``squatter's
right'' of comprehension: \emph{I made it, therefore I knew it}). When agents do
the producing, that automatic byproduct may no longer be generated. The freed-up
time is not guaranteed to be reallocatable to recover the lost understanding; it
is an open question, not an accounting identity.

\medskip
\noindent This reframes a popular slogan. It is often said that
``\emph{verification} is the new bottleneck.'' The sharper claim is that
\textbf{``understanding is the new bottleneck,''} because agents may eventually
verify as competently as they produce --- and yet the human may still not
understand the result. Echoing a classic principle (attributed to IBM): a
machine cannot be held accountable, and therefore must not be trusted to make
decisions. Either agents help us understand the work, or we should not use them.

\section{Practical Method: Checklists and ``Harnesses''}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

To keep production, verification, and understanding aligned, Cunningham
advocates a \textbf{checklist-centered workflow}, or ``harness.'' The rationale
is drawn from Atul Gawande's \emph{The Checklist Manifesto}: in high-stakes
tasks, people skip critical steps --- and, counterintuitively, \emph{experts}
skip steps more often than beginners (a consequence of Kahneman's fast,
``System~1'' thinking). A checklist deliberately re-imposes slow, deliberate
attention. The idea is also latent in the design-based tradition of causal
inference (Rubin's ``Design Trumps Analysis''; the applied checklists of Guido
Imbens and collaborators).

\subsection{Example: A Difference-in-Differences Harness}

A concrete instance is a narrow, difference-in-differences-specific harness.
Deliberately keeping it \emph{narrow} makes it easier to evaluate and perfect.
Selected checklist stages include:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0em, style=nextline]
  \item[Packages.] A visual card recording exactly which packages and
        \emph{versions} the project uses --- guarding against silent drift and
        deprecation.
  \item[Bite.] The first-order, ``boots-on-the-ground'' response to the
        intervention. If a gun law passes, study the \emph{movement of guns}
        first --- not deaths or crime. If a minimum wage rises, find where it
        \emph{binds}. Document the response exhaustively across \emph{space}
        (heat maps) and \emph{time} (time-series aggregations), for treated and
        comparison groups alike, until it is genuinely understood.
\end{description}

The \textbf{bite} stage is where the study is truly \emph{conceived}: it is how
the researcher gains insight into the \emph{treatment assignment mechanism},
which in turn dictates the credible design and the credible comparison group.
This is deliberately the stage \emph{least} suited to full automation. Agents
can and should build the maps, write the code, and produce the figures, but the
human must remain the one who \emph{understands} where, who, and when the
treatment touched the world.

\subsection{Treatment Assignment Mechanisms}

Design-based causal inference (following Angrist and Imbens) emphasizes
understanding \emph{what mechanism assigned the treatment}. The mechanism
selects the appropriate design:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Human rationality / essential heterogeneity} --- agents
        endogenously choose treatment because it is optimal for them
        (Heckman/Roy; Rubin's ``perfect doctor''). Endogenous selection is what
        makes naive causal inference so hard.
  \item \textbf{Instruments} --- an instrument (ideally randomized) assigns
        treatment to compliers, motivating instrumental variables; the exclusion
        restriction additionally forbids any direct effect on the outcome
        (Imbens and Angrist, 1994, LATE).
  \item \textbf{Randomization} --- randomized treatments act directly on the
        outcome (RCTs), collapsing the instrument and the treatment into one.
  \item \textbf{Running variables} --- a threshold rule (e.g.\ a blood-alcohol
        cutoff for a DUI) assigns treatment, motivating regression
        discontinuity (Imbens and Lemieux, ``RDD: A Guide to Practice'').
\end{itemize}

\section{Tools of the Trade: Skills, Hooks, and the Git Diff}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Beyond conversation, Claude Code exposes reusable building blocks that let a
researcher encode a workflow:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Skills} --- packaged, invokable routines that automate a defined
        task (e.g.\ producing a specific exhibit, or wiring figures into a deck
        or manuscript).
  \item \textbf{Hooks} --- automation triggered around actions, ideally made
        \emph{concrete} to each checklist step rather than abstract and
        universal.
  \item \textbf{Markdown project files} --- durable, human-readable instructions
        and context that travel with the project.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Verification via the Git Diff --- and Its Limits}

A popular unit of verification (associated with Paul Goldsmith-Pinkham) is the
\textbf{git diff}: review each change the agent proposes before accepting it. The
danger is that reviewing diffs can decay into \emph{skimming and silently
accepting} them --- accumulating ``cognitive debt'' that the ritual of checking
was supposed to retire. Diffs remain valuable, but they are not sufficient on
their own for genuine understanding.

\subsection{An Emerging Idea: the \texttt{/quiz} Skill}

To attack the understanding bottleneck directly, one experimental approach is a
\texttt{/quiz} skill modeled on corporate training videos: the agent (i)
generates a short set of slides that \emph{teaches} the researcher something
about the current project, then (ii) asks multiple-choice questions one at a
time to confirm comprehension. The aim is \textbf{continuous understanding} and
\textbf{continuous verification} layered on top of the staged checklist --- an
intentional way of ``tying oneself to the mast'' so that attention does not
quietly drift onto autopilot.

\section{Communicating AI-for-Research to Others}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

A pedagogical pattern for presenting agents to non-specialist audiences
(administrators, colleagues, students):

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{History of thought} --- situate the tool (e.g.\ the origin of
        Claude Code as an internal Anthropic tool that later reached a broad
        audience of empiricists via the desktop app).
  \item \textbf{Narrative} --- lead with a concrete personal story.
  \item \textbf{Numerical examples / a live demo} --- the ``magic trick'':
        start from an empty folder and set an agent running a complete
        autonomous study while you lecture; return periodically to show
        progress. It captures attention and viscerally proves that
        \emph{agents are not chatbots}.
  \item \textbf{Interactive media} --- video or hands-on interludes.
  \item \textbf{Technical rigor} --- only now introduce installation, what the
        agent is actually doing (its access to the file system and shell), and
        the conceptual model of skills, hooks, and the production/verification/
        understanding triangle.
\end{enumerate}

The goal is to ``defamiliarize the familiar'' and bring the \emph{median}
attendee forward, not merely the few who would have adopted anyway.

\section{Open Questions and Cautions}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Skill atrophy and the principal--agent problem.} If we trust an
        agent we cannot audit, and our own skills decay, we risk ``the blind
        leading the blind.''
  \item \textbf{Is understanding recoverable?} It is an assumption --- not a
        proven fact --- that time saved by agents can be reinvested to rebuild
        the understanding that hand-production used to generate for free.
  \item \textbf{Reproducibility and disclosure norms.} Requiring authors to
        submit their ``prompts'' or full chat transcripts is argued to be
        both impractical (there is no stable artifact to submit when one
        converses naturally) and an overreach analogous to demanding all
        private communications with human coauthors.
  \item \textbf{Heterogeneity across models.} Whether different frontier models
        differ systematically and predictably in their fitness for autonomous
        research remains an open empirical question.
\end{itemize}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Key Takeaways}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item Coding agents like Claude Code can autonomously \emph{produce} complete
        empirical research; they cannot be trusted to \emph{verify} it or to
        supply human \emph{understanding}.
  \item Organize all agent-assisted research around three doors: \textbf{production,
        verification, understanding}.
  \item Treat \textbf{zero errors as a constraint}, not an objective; act as the
        \textbf{CEO} of the research enterprise.
  \item Agents slash the marginal cost of production, so the true bottleneck
        shifts from output to \textbf{understanding}.
  \item Impose deliberate structure --- \textbf{checklists / harnesses}, skills,
        and hooks --- and keep conception-heavy stages (like \textbf{bite}) under
        human control.
  \item Verify actively (git diffs, and devices like a \texttt{/quiz} skill for
        \textbf{continuous understanding}); do not silently accept.
\end{enumerate}
