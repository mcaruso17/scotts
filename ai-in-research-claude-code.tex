\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Scope.} This document distils the main concepts emerging from a working
economist's use of AI coding agents --- primarily \emph{Claude Code} --- to
conduct causal-inference research. It is a map of ideas, not a how-to manual:
the goal is to make the vocabulary (production, verification, understanding;
drift; hooks; harnesses) explicit and usable.
\end{keybox}

\section{The shift: research at near-zero marginal cost}

AI agents have collapsed the marginal cost of \emph{producing} empirical research
toward zero. An agent can, unattended, choose a research question, pick an
identification strategy, locate and download data, write replicable code, produce
tables and figures with robustness checks, and draft a full manuscript --- an
end-to-end pass sometimes called ``3-D printing a research paper.''

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Economic framing.} If demand for research output slopes downward, a fall in
marginal cost from $MC_{\text{before}}$ to $MC_{\text{AI}}\approx\varepsilon$ raises
the equilibrium quantity of research from $Q^{*}_{b}$ to $Q^{*}_{AI}$ and generates
new surplus. But quantity is not the whole story: the \emph{understanding} that used
to be produced as a by-product of doing the work does not scale automatically with
output.
\end{keybox}

\noindent The crucial caveat: full autonomy is illusory. Present-day agents are less
``self-driving car'' than ``lane-keeping plus cruise control'' --- they extend the
researcher rather than replace them.

\section{The core framework: production, verification, understanding}

The organising idea is that research must pass through \textbf{three distinct doors},
not one:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Production} --- generating the analysis, code, and exhibits.
        \emph{This is solved.} Agents can autonomously and endogenously produce
        empirical work (e.g.\ automated program-evaluation studies at scale).
  \item \textbf{Verification} --- confirming the work is accurate. Replicating code
        or tracing every figure to pipeline code are \emph{necessary but not
        sufficient} conditions. Verification is endogenous to a pre-registered
        target estimand: it must confirm both that what should have been done
        \emph{was} done and that what should \emph{not} have been done was not.
        Agents can assist but should not be the sole verifier.
  \item \textbf{Understanding} --- the human's genuine comprehension of the result.
        This is the emerging bottleneck and cannot be delegated.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Zero errors is a \emph{constraint}, not an objective.} Do not ``minimise''
errors --- that quietly licenses an ``optimal'' non-zero error rate. Instead,
maximise research output \emph{subject to} a zero-error constraint that requires
both verification and understanding.
\end{keybox}

\section{Understanding is the new bottleneck}

A popular slogan holds that ``verification is the new bottleneck.'' The sharper claim
is that \textbf{understanding} is the bottleneck: agents may soon verify as well as
they produce, yet that still does not guarantee the human understands the result.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Understanding as an externality.} Historically, comprehension was an
        accidental by-product of doing the work by hand --- like pollution from
        production. Remove the manual labour and the by-product may simply vanish;
        the recovered time does not automatically refill it.
  \item \textbf{``Memory is the residue of thought.''} Writing one's own code builds
        project-specific human capital --- you know where the code is, what it means,
        the feel of its syntax. Automating the task erases the thought, and with it
        the memory and the local productivity advantage it conferred.
  \item \textbf{Cognitive debt.} Reviewing agent output via the \texttt{git diff} as
        the ``unit of verification'' helps, but skimming and silently accepting diffs
        accrues cognitive debt rather than clearing it.
\end{itemize}

\section{Principal--agent problems and ``drift''}

Working with an agent is a principal--agent relationship. The agent takes
\textbf{hidden actions} --- cost-minimising shortcuts within its discretion --- that a
skilled principal notices and an unskilled one does not.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Drift} is any time the agent does its own thing despite written instructions:
producing a figure outside the replicable pipeline, ``vibe-coding'' a long computation
straight in the terminal with no saved script, or spawning new files instead of
editing existing ones (leaving stale, unreplicable code). Each caught instance raises
the haunting question: \emph{how many did I not catch?}
\end{keybox}

\section{Three levels of control: markdowns, skills, hooks}

Prose does not constrain agents --- it neither reliably causes good behaviour nor
prevents bad behaviour, producing false positives and false negatives at scale. The
governing metaphor is a government with three branches:

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l l X@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Mechanism} & \textbf{Branch} & \textbf{What it is / its limit} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Markdown} & Legislative & \texttt{CLAUDE.md}, README, progress logs, prompts.
  States the rules but \emph{assumes} the agent reads and obeys them. \\
\textbf{Skills} & Judicial & Invocable procedures (e.g.\ \texttt{/quiz},
  \texttt{/referee2}). Assume the \emph{human} remembers to call them. \\
\textbf{Hooks} & \textbf{Executive} & Scripts that intercept a tool call and allow or
  deny it \emph{before it happens}. The only truly \emph{enforceable} layer. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\noindent A civilised research process needs an executive branch: laws (markdown) and
courts (skills) are not enough without enforcement (hooks).

\section{Hooks: enforceable constraints on agent behaviour}

An agent has ``no hands'': every action passes through a small set of named tools
(\texttt{Read}, \texttt{Write}, \texttt{Edit}, \texttt{Bash}). Each call is announced
in advance as a structured packet (the tool plus its arguments). A \textbf{hook} is a
script standing at that announcement that reads the packet and answers \emph{yes} or
\emph{no}.

\subsection{Two mechanics}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Pre-action hooks} run \emph{before} the action and can cancel it ---
        a locked door that no sign or prose can talk its way through.
  \item \textbf{Post-action hooks} run \emph{after} and shout, so the problem is fixed
        in the same sitting rather than days later.
  \item \textbf{Fail open.} If the hook's own code breaks, it lets the researcher
        through rather than freezing the workflow --- a deliberate safety guard against
        over-engineering.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Worked example: \texttt{protect-raw-data}}
The workflow rests on one axiom: \emph{the original data is untouchable}. The hook
registers only against \emph{mutating} tools (\texttt{Write}, \texttt{Edit},
\texttt{Bash}) and inspects one field --- the target directory path. If the path lies
under \texttt{/data/raw}, the call is denied with an instruction to write to
\texttt{/data/clean} instead. \texttt{Read} is deliberately left open: the promise is
not ``nobody looks at the raw data'' but ``the original file is the same today as it
was in June.''

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Why the hook succeeds where the markdown rule failed.} The prose rule required
the agent to \emph{hold a fact in mind} while busy with something else. The hook asks
one mechanical question --- does the path start with \texttt{raw/}? --- on every single
write, forever. Nothing has to be remembered by anyone. This converts ``remind me not
to'' into ``make it impossible to.'' A representative hook set: \texttt{protect-raw-data},
\texttt{no-fabricated-exhibit}, \texttt{no-off-book-exhibit}, \texttt{deck-from-pipeline},
\texttt{no-stale-canon}.
\end{keybox}

\section{Harnesses, checklists, and human-in-the-loop design}

Beyond single hooks, the researcher builds a \textbf{harness} --- a structured,
often visual checklist that routes a project through production, verification, and
understanding for one method (e.g.\ difference-in-differences).

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Checklists (à la Gawande's \emph{Checklist Manifesto}).} Experts skip
        steps more than novices --- ``thinking fast'' (Kahneman's System~1). A checklist
        re-imposes the skipped steps on high-stakes work.
  \item \textbf{The ``bite'' stage should \emph{not} be fully automated.} Before
        estimation, exhaustively document the treatment's first-stage footprint on the
        world (heat maps, time series, comparison groups). Agents can \emph{make} these
        exhibits, but the researcher must \emph{understand} the treatment-assignment
        mechanism --- human rationality/selection, instruments, randomisation, or a
        running variable --- because that choice determines a credible design.
  \item \textbf{Deliberate friction / the \texttt{/quiz} skill.} To rebuild
        understanding, the agent generates teaching slides and then quizzes the
        researcher with multiple-choice questions --- ``corporate training video''
        pedagogy turned on oneself, layered on top of diffs and checklists.
\end{itemize}

\section{Practical principles}

\begin{keybox}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Everything with agents must be on purpose.} Reduced thinking is fine
        if chosen deliberately; if it happens unnoticed, it is a principal--agent
        problem demanding attention.
  \item \textbf{Pre-commit to structure.} Hooks only work atop conventions decided in
        advance (e.g.\ \texttt{/data/raw} vs.\ \texttt{/data/clean}, numbered pipeline code).
  \item \textbf{Prefer impossibility to reminders.} For fatal errors, drive the
        probability to zero with a hook rather than lowering it with prose.
  \item \textbf{Treat sessions as amnesiac.} Each session is effectively a new agent;
        leave breadcrumbs (markdown, progress logs) so work carries over.
  \item \textbf{Keep the human in the cockpit.} Let agents co-pilot creative,
        learning-rich work; resist letting them autopilot unattended.
\end{enumerate}
\end{keybox}
