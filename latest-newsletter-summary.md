## Main topics / themes

- **How AI extends the same story** — why the labor-economics of technology matters for the AI era.

## Key points & insights

- Scott predicts **David Autor and Larry Katz** win, honored for work on **skill-biased technological change** (possibly with Kevin Murphy sharing for early contributions).
- He distinguishes two "core ideas" in economics: the **wealth of nations** (production, technology, growth) versus the **wages of nations** (allocation and distribution of that output).
- The **SBTC mechanism**: new technology (e.g. computers) raises skilled workers' marginal product, lifting demand and wages for college graduates and widening the education wage premium.
- **Katz & Murphy** framed wages as a "race" between the *demand* for skill (driven by technology) and the *supply* of skill (driven by education); **Goldin & Katz** traced this race across the whole 20th century.
- **Card & DiNardo's critique** (inequality plateaued in the 1990s while computers kept spreading) spurred the pivotal move from "skills" to **tasks**: Autor, Levy & Murnane asked what computers actually *do* — routine tasks — seeding the **task model**.
- The task model underpins the **automation / job-polarization ("hollowing out" of the middle class)** literature — routine cognitive tasks are automatable, non-routine cognitive tasks are not — documented by Autor, Katz & Kearney, and extended by Acemoglu & Restrepo.
- Autor's **"China shock"** work (with Dorn & Hanson) is cited as a second major contribution showing how trade exposure reshaped local labor markets.
- Scott pre-empts the objection that the committee already rewarded technology-and-growth (Mokyr, Aghion, Howitt): that prize was about *why economies grow*; this one would be about *who wins and loses* from technological change — a distinct, distributional question.
- He argues the prize would also **anticipate AI**, which — unlike earlier computers — may automate *cognitive, non-routine* work, keeping the education-vs-technology race central.
- His **second guess ("write-in")**: **Berry, Levinsohn & Pakes (BLP)** for structural industrial-organization demand models.
- A lighter thread: his mixed **track record** — he "basically called" Card/Angrist/Imbens and got close on Acemoglu/Johnson/Robinson, but "airballed" a Goldin/Katz year after letting ChatGPT make a last-minute pick.
