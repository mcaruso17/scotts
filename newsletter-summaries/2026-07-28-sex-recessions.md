## Main topics / themes
- The shift of heterosexual matching from **information-rich "friend platforms" to information-thin online-dating "stranger" platforms** (the ~2012 crossover, coinciding with Tinder/Hinge).
- A simple **three-risks framework** for dating: unwanted pregnancy (`u`), STI (`s`), and deceptive/violent match (`d`).
- The rise of **screening / whisper-network mechanisms** as pregnancy and STI risk fall.
- The apparent paradox of a **"sex recession"** alongside **rising casual sex**.
- Falling **trust** (generalized trust and religious attendance) as a backdrop.
- A preview of a new project, tentatively titled **"Strangers."**

## Key points / insights
- Online dating is now the **modal** way heterosexual couples meet (~45%), overtaking friends around 2012 — the year Tinder launched.
- The deeper change isn't just the platform switch but that matching is now between **strangers** with only thin, curated profile information (hence catfishing and verification).
- Historically, friend-platforms were information-rich, so pregnancy and STI risk dominated over match risk (`u + s > d`); trust could substitute for screening.
- LARC, abortion access, HAART, and PrEP have pushed **pregnancy and STI risk to negligible levels**, leaving **deceptive/violent-match risk** as the main residual concern.
- Moving online **mechanically raises `d`**, so participants substitute toward **screening tools** rather than contraception.
- **"Are We Dating the Same Guy?" (AWDSG)** Facebook groups — started NYC, March 2022 — spread to millions of members, filling the informational gap left by the end of friend-platforms.
- General Social Survey data show the **share reporting no sex in the past year rising** (earlier/higher for men, ~2012–13), the basis for "sex recession" headlines.
- Conditional on being sexually active, the share reporting a **casual partner has risen to ~60–70%**, higher for men — a statistic the author hadn't seen highlighted before.
- The casual-sex rise is concentrated among the **unmarried** and pivots up circa 2012–13 (not among married couples).
- The author argues these regularities are **under-discussed** in economics debates on fertility, technology, and online dating — it's not only that matching moved online, but that the whole matching technology was replaced amid collapsing trust and medical/policy risk reduction.
