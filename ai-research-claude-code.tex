\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This document summarizes the main concepts behind using AI agents in
research, with an emphasis on \term{Claude Code}---a command-line coding
agent---as a tool for applied empirical work. It draws on the ongoing
essay series by economist Scott Cunningham, who has documented a
book-length, real-time experiment in conducting causal-inference research
with an AI agent. The recurring themes are economic in spirit: AI lowers
the marginal cost of producing research, which shifts the binding
constraint away from \emph{production} and toward \emph{verification} and,
more subtly, \emph{understanding}. The practical response is a set of
disciplined workflows---staged checklists, reproducibility guarantees, and
active-learning routines---designed to keep a human researcher in genuine
command of work an agent largely produces.
\end{abstract}

\section{Why AI Changes the Economics of Research}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The cleanest way to frame AI's effect on research is as a shift in the
\term{marginal cost of producing research}. Before capable AI agents, the
cost of producing an additional unit of research output was effectively
fixed by a researcher's time and human capital; output was chosen where
the marginal benefit of research equalled that marginal cost.

AI agents push the marginal cost of production down toward a small
$\varepsilon$ above zero. Two things follow:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \term{More research gets produced.} As marginal cost falls from
        $MC_b$ (``before AI'') to $\widehat{MC}_{AI}\approx\varepsilon$, the
        equilibrium quantity rises from $Q^*_b$ to $Q^*_{AI}$. How much it
        rises depends on the elasticity of research demand.
  \item \term{New surplus appears.} Old work is now done with far fewer
        time inputs, and additional work becomes worthwhile that previously
        was not---generating surpluses that did not exist before.
\end{itemize}

The provocative part is what happens to the \emph{value} of the old work.
Historically, producing a piece of research and \emph{understanding} it
were nearly the same act: understanding was a prerequisite to doing the
work and a near-automatic byproduct of having done it. When an agent
produces the work instead, that automatic understanding is no longer
guaranteed. The essay frames this as understanding possibly being an
\term{externality} of human production---something created ``by accident''
in the course of doing the work, the way pollution is a byproduct of
manufacturing. If so, the time an agent saves cannot be assumed to
automatically refill the lost understanding.

\section{The Central Question: What Is Now the Bottleneck?}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Two competing slogans organize the current debate:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0em, itemsep=0.4em]
  \item[``Verification is the new bottleneck.''] If agents produce
        cheaply, the scarce human task becomes checking that the output is
        correct.
  \item[``Understanding is the new bottleneck.''] A stronger claim
        (associated with a Geoffrey Litt essay of that title): agents may
        eventually \emph{verify} as well as they produce, yet a human still
        may not \emph{understand} the result. Verification and
        understanding are not the same thing.
\end{description}

Cunningham argues the second framing is more accurate. One can experience
a result as valuable because one believes it is correct, while whether it
is \emph{actually} correct---and socially valuable---is a separate matter.
Closing that gap is the real challenge, and it is not obviously solved by
having more free time.

\subsection{The principal--agent problem in research}
Delegating to an agent recreates a classic \term{principal--agent}
problem. The researcher (principal) trusts the agent to have done the work
well but cannot directly observe effort or competence---and, crucially, the
agent ``is not real'' and cannot be held accountable. A frequently cited
formulation (attributed to IBM) captures the stakes: \emph{a machine cannot
be held accountable, therefore a machine must never make a management
decision}. The corollary for research: an agent must actively help the
human understand the work, or it should not be relied upon at all.

\subsection{Skill atrophy and cognitive debt}
Two related risks compound the problem:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \term{Skill atrophy.} If a researcher stops doing the work, their
        ability to judge whether it was ``done well'' may decay---``the
        blind leading the blind.''
  \item \term{Cognitive debt.} Reviewing an agent's changes as a stream of
        \term{git diffs} (Paul Goldsmith-Pinkham's ``the unit of
        verification is the git diff'') is meant to discharge the debt of
        understanding one diff at a time. In practice it is easy to skim
        and silently accept diffs, accumulating debt rather than clearing
        it.
\end{itemize}

\section{Guiding Principles for Research with AI Agents}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em, itemsep=0.45em]
  \item \term{Zero errors is the \emph{constraint}, not the objective
        function.} Treating error minimization as the goal quietly
        smuggles in the notion of an ``optimal'' non-zero error rate. When
        research output is highly elastic with respect to falling costs,
        even a small accepted error rate scales into a large absolute
        number of errors. Errors are a hard constraint to satisfy, not a
        quantity to trade off.
  \item \term{Understanding is (also) a constraint.} Beyond correctness, a
        threshold level of human understanding of the work should be
        treated as a requirement the workflow must meet.
  \item \term{Optimal research output is the objective function.} The
        target is the socially optimal quantity of research---where social
        marginal benefit equals social marginal cost---which is only
        meaningful once verification and understanding are guaranteed.
  \item \term{Keep the human in the cockpit.} The preferred mode is the
        agent as co-pilot that helps the researcher learn, not an
        autopilot that runs while the researcher sleeps. ``Tie yourself to
        the mast'': build guardrails that force engagement precisely
        because skimming is so tempting.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Practical Workflows and Techniques}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The essays translate these principles into concrete, repeatable practices.

\subsection{Staged, detailed checklists}
Rather than issuing open-ended prompts, work is decomposed into explicit,
staged checklists. Each stage is small enough to verify and is checked off
deliberately, which keeps the analytical pipeline legible and auditable.

\subsection{Verification via git diffs---plus something more}
Diff-by-diff review remains the backbone of verification, but on its own it
invites passive acceptance. The proposed remedy is to add an
\emph{understanding} layer on top of verification, so the goal becomes two
things at once: confirm the change is correct \emph{and} confirm the human
grasps it.

\subsection{The \texttt{/quiz} skill: active-learning by the agent}
A notable experiment is a custom \term{Claude Code skill} called
\texttt{/quiz}. It has two parts:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item The agent generates a short set of \term{slides} that teach the
        researcher something about the current work.
  \item The agent then asks \term{multiple-choice questions}, one at a
        time, based on those slides.
\end{enumerate}
The design is explicitly modeled on corporate ``HR training video +
quiz'' modules: read/watch, then test. The aim is to manufacture
\emph{continuous understanding} deliberately, replacing the understanding
that used to arrive as a free byproduct of doing the work by hand. It
extends an earlier practice of \term{interviews}---the agent interrogating
the researcher to elicit their beliefs about, e.g., which covariates to
include or what the target parameter should be.

\subsection{Reproducibility and guarding against ``stale code''}
A recurring failure mode is silent drift: an analytical sample changing
from $N$ to $N+k$ observations without a clear, traceable reason. The
antidote is a \term{structured, reproducible workflow}---a canonical,
end-to-end runnable pipeline---so that code cannot silently deprecate and
every number in the paper can be regenerated from source. This is where
much of the value of a coding agent lies: it makes full reproducibility
cheap enough to do by default.

\subsection{Tooling and integration}
The agent operates as a command-line tool with access to the project's
files and can be extended through custom skills and integrations (for
example, an MCP integration that connects the agent to external services
such as Substack). The general pattern is to give the agent structured,
scoped access to the artifacts of the research rather than treating it as a
disembodied chatbot.

\section{A Running Applied Example}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The concepts are grounded in a real project: estimating the effect of
Texas House Bill 2---which closed roughly half the state's abortion
clinics---on marriage rates, using a difference-in-differences design with
a ``continuous treatment'' (distance to the nearest clinic). Documented
across a multi-episode series, the agent-assisted workflow covers the
ordinary hard parts of applied causal inference: probing the
\term{parallel-trends} assumption, hunting for \term{confounders} that
could threaten identification, constructing the estimator, and iterating
toward a defensible specification. The episodes make the abstract argument
tangible---the agent accelerates production dramatically, while the
researcher's job shifts toward interrogating assumptions and guaranteeing
that the accelerated output is both correct and understood.

\section{Broader Themes: AI Applications in Research}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Stepping back from the specific tool, the same ideas generalize across
research domains:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=0.35em]
  \item \term{Agentic coding assistants} lower the cost of data cleaning,
        estimation, simulation, robustness checks, and visualization,
        expanding how much analysis a single researcher can credibly run.
  \item \term{Reproducibility as a first-class output.} Because agents work
        directly with code and version control, fully reproducible
        pipelines become the cheap default rather than a costly aspiration.
  \item \term{Verification tooling matters more than raw generation.} The
        binding constraint moves downstream, so investment should flow to
        tests, diffs, checklists, and audit trails.
  \item \term{Human capital is reallocated, not eliminated.} The scarce
        human skills become framing the question, choosing the
        identification strategy, judging assumptions, and---increasingly---%
        maintaining genuine understanding of machine-produced results.
  \item \term{Research integrity and trust.} Delegation raises the stakes
        of accountability: the field's norms around who is responsible for
        an error do not disappear simply because an agent produced it.
\end{itemize}

\section{Key Takeaways}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em, itemsep=0.35em]
  \item AI agents collapse the marginal cost of \emph{producing} research;
        the scarce resource becomes \emph{verifying} and, above all,
        \emph{understanding} it.
  \item Understanding may have been an accidental byproduct of doing the
        work by hand; when an agent does the work, understanding must be
        engineered on purpose.
  \item ``Zero errors'' should be a binding constraint, not a quantity to
        optimize; understanding should be a constraint too.
  \item Practical discipline---staged checklists, diff review plus an
        understanding layer, reproducible pipelines, and active-learning
        routines like a \texttt{/quiz} skill---is what keeps a human in real
        command.
  \item The healthiest posture is the agent as \emph{co-pilot} that helps
        the researcher keep learning, not an autopilot that hollows out the
        researcher's judgment.
\end{enumerate}
