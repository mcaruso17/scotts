\begin{abstract}
\noindent This document summarises the main concepts emerging from a body of
recent writing on the use of AI agents---especially \textbf{Claude Code}---in
quantitative empirical research. The central claim is that frontier coding
agents can now \emph{produce} contemporary empirical research end-to-end, but
that production is only one of three tasks a credible researcher owes: research
must also pass through \emph{verification} and \emph{understanding}. The
document organises the practitioner tooling of Claude Code (context files,
skills, hooks, connectors, and the Model Context Protocol) around this
production--verification--understanding framework, and around a design-based,
checklist-driven philosophy of causal inference.
\end{abstract}

\section{The new economics of research production}
% ===============================================================

Before AI agents, the marginal cost of producing a unit of research was set by
the researcher's scarce time and human capital. A researcher produced up to the
point where the marginal benefit of an additional unit equalled that marginal
cost. Frontier agents collapse this marginal cost toward an arbitrarily small
$\varepsilon > 0$:
\[
  MC_{\text{before}} \;\longrightarrow\; \widehat{MC}_{\text{AI}} \approx \varepsilon .
\]
Two consequences follow. First, the \emph{quantity} of research produced rises
(from $Q^{*}_{b}$ to $Q^{*}_{\text{AI}}$). Second, work that would have been done
anyway is now done with far fewer time inputs, generating additional surplus.

\begin{keyidea}
Cheaper production does not automatically preserve the \emph{understanding} that
used to be a by-product of doing the work by hand. The old surplus bundled
production and understanding together; when the agent produces, that bundling
can break.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{Understanding is the new bottleneck}
A popular slogan holds that ``verification is the new bottleneck.'' A sharper
statement is that \textbf{understanding is the new bottleneck}: agents may be
able to verify as well as produce, yet the human can still fail to understand
what was done. Historically, understanding arrived almost as an
\emph{externality} of production---you could not build a result without
comprehending it along the way. Once the agent does the building, that
externality is no longer automatic and must be deliberately re-created.

% ===============================================================
\section{The production--verification--understanding triangle}
% ===============================================================

The organising framework for doing research with agents is a triangle of three
distinct ``doors'' every research output must pass through:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.6em, style=nextline]
  \item[\textbf{Production.}] Agents can now autonomously and endogenously
  \emph{produce} empirical research: choose a question and identification
  strategy, locate and download data, pick estimators, write replicable code,
  generate tables and figures with robustness checks, and draft the manuscript.
  This capability is no longer conjecture---it has been demonstrated at scale
  (e.g.\ an autonomous ``program evaluation'' project that produced roughly
  1{,}000 empirical papers using difference-in-differences, instrumental
  variables, and related designs). The colloquial description is
  ``3-D printing a research paper'' in under 30 minutes.
  \item[\textbf{Verification.}] Agents should \emph{not} be relied on
  exclusively to verify their own work. Replicating code in several languages or
  confirming that every exhibit traces to stored code are \emph{necessary but
  not sufficient} conditions. Verification is endogenous to a pre-stated target
  estimand: it means confirming that what \emph{should} have been done was done
  \emph{and that what should not have been done was not}. The latter has no
  artifact for an agent to check---so it falls to the human.
  \item[\textbf{Understanding.}] The researcher is the \emph{CEO} of the
  enterprise: delegation is fine, but the CEO must still know, in an
  approximately correct sense, what was done, by whom, and what was found. A
  single manuscript is a convenient ``sufficient statistic'' for all three
  doors.
\end{description}

\begin{keyidea}
\textbf{Zero errors is a constraint, not the objective function.} If we merely
``minimise'' errors we implicitly accept an \emph{optimal} positive error rate.
Instead, treat zero errors as a hard constraint in the Lagrangian of producing a
manuscript that has passed production, verification, and understanding.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{Autonomy: assisted driving, not full self-driving}
Although an agent can drive a paper start to finish, in practice it behaves more
like lane-keeping plus cruise control than a fully autonomous vehicle. A
documented failure mode is \emph{endogenous specification search}: agents can
drift into model specifications not because they were instructed to, but in
response to true statements made in the research context. This is why
verification and understanding cannot be delegated wholesale.

\section{Governing the agent: markdown, skills, and hooks}
% ===============================================================

A recurring problem is \textbf{drift}: an agent quietly does its own thing
despite written instructions---taking cost-minimising shortcuts, editing source
data, ``vibe-coding'' a result straight in the terminal with no saved script, or
proliferating stale duplicate files. Prose alone (prompts and \texttt{CLAUDE.md})
neither reliably \emph{causes} good behaviour nor reliably \emph{prevents} bad
behaviour. Three governance mechanisms can be arranged in a $3\times3$ matrix
along the axes \emph{who runs it}, \emph{can it be skipped}, and \emph{when is it
chosen}:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.8em]
  \item \textbf{Markdown / context files} (\texttt{CLAUDE.md}, README, progress
  logs). Persistent memory and intent across sessions---but they \emph{assume}
  the agent will read and obey them. Legislative branch: they write the law.
  \item \textbf{Skills} (e.g.\ \texttt{/referee2}, \texttt{/quiz},
  \texttt{/beautiful\_deck}). Reusable, invocable procedures---but they assume
  the \emph{human} remembers to call them. Judicial branch: they interpret and
  apply.
  \item \textbf{Hooks.} Scripts that intercept tool calls and \emph{enforce}
  behaviour deterministically. Executive branch: they actually halt or repair
  actions.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{keyidea}
Markdown and skills are advisory; only \textbf{hooks} are enforceable. To use
agents seriously for research you need an executive branch, not just a
legislative and judicial one.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{How a hook works}
An agent has ``no hands'': every action passes through a small set of named tools
(\texttt{Read}, \texttt{Edit}, \texttt{Write}, \texttt{Bash}, \dots), each
announced in advance as a structured packet of ``tool + arguments.'' A hook
stands at that announcement, inspects one or more fields, and answers
\emph{allow} or \emph{deny} before anything happens. Two mechanics matter:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{Pre-action hooks} behave like a \emph{locked door}: the action is
  blocked regardless of any sign (prose) explaining the rule.
  \item \textbf{Post-action hooks} run after the fact and \emph{shout}, so the
  problem is fixed in the same sitting rather than days later.
  \item \textbf{Fail-open safety.} If the hook's own code breaks, it lets the
  work through rather than freezing the researcher out.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Worked example --- \texttt{protect-raw-data}.} The axiom ``the raw
data is untouchable'' underwrites every downstream number. A markdown rule asks
the agent to \emph{remember} this while busy doing something else; a hook makes
violation \emph{impossible}. The hook registers against mutating verbs
(\texttt{Write}, \texttt{Edit}, \texttt{Bash}) only, reads a single field---the
target path---and denies any write under \texttt{/data/raw}, redirecting it to a
derived folder such as \texttt{/data/clean}. Crucially, \texttt{Read} is left
open on purpose: the promise being enforced is not ``nobody looks'' but ``the
original file is the same today as it was in June.''

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\textbf{An illustrative hook set for empirical research}}\\[2pt]
\texttt{protect-raw-data}   & block edits to the raw-data directory \\
\texttt{no-fabricated-exhibit} & forbid tables/figures not traced to code \\
\texttt{no-off-book-exhibit}   & forbid exhibits produced outside the pipeline \\
\texttt{deck-from-pipeline}    & decks may only draw on pipelined outputs \\
\texttt{no-stale-canon}        & prevent stale duplicate ``canonical'' files \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{The replicable pipeline}
Underlying the hooks is a discipline: every number, table, and figure must trace
back to numbered, stored, re-runnable code in a code subdirectory---never to an
ephemeral terminal session. Raw data in \texttt{/data/raw}; derived data in
\texttt{/data/clean}; exhibits generated only by pipeline scripts. This layout is
a precondition for hooks to be able to enforce anything at all.

\section{Connectors and the Model Context Protocol (MCP)}
% ===============================================================

\textbf{Connectors} extend an agent beyond the local machine into external and
cloud services, and make the same capabilities reachable from the phone-based
Claude app---not just the desktop Claude Code environment. They are powered by
the \textbf{Model Context Protocol (MCP)}, an open standard that lets AI
applications connect securely to external data sources and tools to read
real-time information and take actions---``a new kind of piping'' into cloud
services.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item A large and growing directory (an ``App Store'' for connectors), spanning
  categories such as code, communication, and financial services.
  \item \textbf{Community connectors} are user-created and only lightly vetted---
  useful but ``buyer beware.''
  \item Illustrative research/productivity uses: searching a Dropbox from the
  phone; wiring in accounting/payroll tools; enabling ``solo-entrepreneur''
  workflows in which one person runs a small business with agent support.
\end{itemize}

For empirical research specifically, connectors are promising but not yet a
settled part of the workflow; the payoff is expected to come from cloud services
that are only efficiently reachable through MCP.

\section{A design-based, checklist-driven harness for causal inference}
% ===============================================================

The proposed way to keep agents disciplined is a \textbf{harness}: an
organisational workflow---increasingly, a piece of software with a visual
dashboard---that stages empirical work through production, verification, and
understanding. Its intellectual backbone is the \emph{checklist}, following
Gawande's \emph{Checklist Manifesto}: experts skip steps precisely because they
think fast (Kahneman's System~1), so high-stakes work needs explicit checklists.
The same checklist instinct runs through the design-based tradition (Rubin's
``Design Trumps Analysis''; Imbens' practitioner guides).

\subsection{Treatment assignment mechanisms}
Design-based inference emphasises \emph{how} the treatment was assigned, because
the assignment mechanism selects the appropriate estimator:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em, itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Human rationality / essential heterogeneity} (endogenous choice)
  --- the hardest case; endogeneity can make naive causal inference invalid.
  \item \textbf{Instruments} --- randomised assignment buried in the instrument
  $\Rightarrow$ instrumental variables / LATE (subject to the exclusion
  restriction).
  \item \textbf{Randomised treatment} --- the RCT, where randomisation acts
  directly on the treatment.
  \item \textbf{Running variables} --- a threshold rule $\Rightarrow$ regression
  discontinuity design.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The ``bite'' stage --- keep the human in the loop}
An early checklist stage is \textbf{bite}: exhaustively document the very first
real-world response to the intervention---across \emph{space} (maps, heat maps of
first-stage take-up) and \emph{time} (time-series aggregations), for treated and
comparison units alike. Agents can \emph{produce} the maps, code, and figures,
but bite is deliberately \emph{not} fully automated: it is where the project is
conceived, where credibility is established, and where the analyst forms a
decisive view of the assignment mechanism. It is the clearest case of keeping a
human in the loop.

\begin{keyidea}
Automate the mechanical production of exhibits (verification-friendly work); keep
the human firmly in the loop for conception and interpretation (the
understanding-critical work).
\end{keyidea}

% ===============================================================
\section{Techniques for keeping the human in the loop}
% ===============================================================

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{The \texttt{git diff} as the unit of verification.} Reviewing
  each diff is the atomic act of verification---but passively skimming and
  accepting diffs builds \emph{cognitive debt} and only simulates understanding.
  \item \textbf{The \texttt{/quiz} skill.} To force genuine comprehension, have
  the agent generate teaching slides and then quiz the researcher with
  one-at-a-time multiple-choice questions (modelled on corporate
  ``training-video quizzes''), layered on top of verification and staged
  checklists.
  \item \textbf{Interviews to elicit priors.} Structured interviews extract the
  researcher's beliefs about which covariates to include and what the target
  estimand should be, before estimation.
  \item \textbf{Visual staging.} Interactive, ``flippable-card'' dashboards (and
  even spatial/AR interfaces) help the researcher hold the state of a
  large project in mind---restoring the ``seeing'' that hand-written code used to
  provide.
\end{itemize}

% ===============================================================
\section{Takeaways}
% ===============================================================

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.8em]
  \item AI agents (Claude Code) drive the \emph{marginal cost of research toward
  zero} and can autonomously produce complete empirical manuscripts.
  \item Production is necessary but not sufficient: research must also pass
  \emph{verification} and \emph{understanding}; \textbf{understanding is the
  binding constraint}.
  \item \textbf{Zero errors} is a constraint, not an objective; do not settle for
  ``optimal'' error rates.
  \item Govern agents with the right tool for the job: markdown (intent), skills
  (procedures), and---decisively---\textbf{hooks} (enforcement).
  \item Keep a \textbf{replicable pipeline} and protect raw data by construction,
  not by reminder.
  \item Use \textbf{connectors / MCP} to extend agents into external and cloud
  services, with care around unvetted community connectors.
  \item Anchor everything in a \textbf{design-based, checklist-driven harness};
  automate exhibit production but keep the human in the loop for conception,
  interpretation, and the ``bite'' stage.
\end{enumerate}
