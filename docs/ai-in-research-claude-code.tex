\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This document summarises the main concepts around using AI agents --- and
\concept{Claude Code} in particular --- for quantitative and empirical research,
as developed across the 56+ essays economist Scott Cunningham has published on
his newsletter since November 2025. The central claim is that agentic AI does
not merely speed up existing research tasks; it changes the \emph{production
function} of research itself, shifting the scarce resource from producing
cognitive output to \emph{verifying} it, and thereby demanding new workflows,
harnesses, and curation rituals. The concepts below are organised as a small
theory of how careful empirical researchers can capture the gains from these
tools while controlling their characteristic failure modes.
\end{abstract}

\section{The Core Economic Reframing}

Cunningham frames AI agents through the lens of production economics rather than
software tooling.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.4em, style=nextline]
  \item[The production isoquant.] A single unit of ``cognitive output'' --- a
    regression table, a figure, a paragraph, or an entire manuscript --- can be
    produced with different ratios of \concept{machine time ($M$)} to
    \concept{human time ($H$)}. Historically the isoquant was steeply
    \emph{human-time intensive}: the only machine time was, say, waiting for
    Stata to invert a matrix.
  \item[Perfect substitutes.] With agents such as Claude Code, the isoquants
    ``straighten out'' toward linearity. Machine and human time become closer to
    \emph{perfect substitutes}, so the cost-minimising equilibrium pushes the
    mix as close to the machine-time intercept $M^\ast$ as possible.
  \item[Falling marginal cost of production.] Because agents lower the price of
    producing output, quantity rises --- more projects started, more papers
    drafted, deeper exploration. Cunningham reports doing work he ``would not
    have started'' otherwise.
\end{description}

\subsection{Production is not verification}

The pivotal insight is that the \concept{production function is not the
verification function}. Lowering the cost of \emph{producing} output does not
lower the cost of confirming that the output is \emph{correct}, or even that it
is what the researcher intended. Agents therefore drive a widening
\concept{wedge between production and verification}: output volume explodes while
the human's ability to check it does not scale, so \emph{aggregate errors can
rise even as average quality per unit is high}. Verification, not production,
becomes the binding constraint on serious research.

\section{The Researcher as ``CEO of the Kitchen''}

To describe the shift in the researcher's role, Cunningham uses the metaphor of
a Michelin restaurant (Amelia, San Sebasti\'an). The head and executive chefs
physically make the food, yet every apron bears the name of the
\emph{founder}, who no longer cooks. The founder is the \concept{CEO of the
kitchen}: without him no meal is made, even though he makes none of them.

\medskip
\noindent
The analogy: when Claude Code produces the code, tables, and prose, the
researcher stops being the ``line cook'' and becomes the accountable author and
curator --- responsible for the output, its correctness, and its meaning,
without having typed most of it. Authorship becomes an act of
\emph{direction and verification} rather than of manual production.

\section{Characteristic Failure Mode: ``AI Agent Drift''}

Agentic research introduces a signature pathology Cunningham calls
\concept{AI Agent drift}:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Sample drift.} On a long project the analytical sample silently
    changed from $N$ to $N+k$ observations with no explanation --- caught only
    because of a disciplined habit of regenerating ``beautiful tables and
    figures.''
  \item \textbf{File / code proliferation.} What the researcher assumed was
    in-place editing of one program turned out to be $\sim\!5K$ near-duplicate
    programs with confusing, similar-sounding names --- a return of the old
    ``copies of the same code with weird naming conventions'' problem, now
    generated at machine speed and at greater distance from the author.
  \item \textbf{Forgetful improvisation.} The agent improvises without full
    awareness of prior state, so results and samples can shift between sessions
    unless the process is tightly harnessed.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Response: A Rigid Harness and Workflow}

Drift is treated as a \concept{design problem}, not a discipline problem
(echoing Don Rubin's dictum that ``design trumps analysis''). The remedy is an
explicit \concept{harness}: a rigid, ritualised checklist plus a dashboard that
makes state visible. Cunningham's causal-inference checklist runs to roughly
13--14 mandatory stages:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}rll@{}}
\toprule
\# & Stage & Purpose \\
\midrule
1  & \texttt{PKG}        & Declare all software packages used up front \\
2  & \texttt{TARGET}     & Define the causal estimand in potential-outcomes terms \\
3  & \texttt{BITE}       & Document the treatment's real-world ``bite'' (2--3 exhibits) \\
4  & \texttt{COVARIATES} & Select controls for unconfoundedness / parallel trends \\
5  & \texttt{ROLLOUT}    & Document treatment timing and cohorts (e.g.\ \texttt{panelview}) \\
6  & \texttt{OUTCOME}    & Present the outcome to match the intended analysis \\
7  & \texttt{POWER}      & Power calculations, minimum detectable effect \\
8  & \texttt{ESTIMATOR}  & Choose and estimate, flowing from TARGET + BITE \\
9  & \texttt{EVENT}      & Produce event-study graphics \\
10 & \texttt{FALSIFY}    & Run falsification / placebo tests \\
11 & \texttt{MECHANISM}  & Investigate candidate mechanisms \\
12 & \texttt{PIPELINE}   & Re-run end to end; confirm replication \\
13 & \texttt{DELIVER}    & Storyboard and draft the findings \\
14 & \texttt{SIGN}       & Sign off \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{The dashboard: locks, colours, and ``you are here''}

Each stage is a \concept{lock} on a visual dashboard, rendered as a row of
colour-coded circles:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textcolor{green!55!black}{\textbf{green}} = closed (verified and complete);
  \item \textcolor{red!75!black}{\textbf{red}} = open (not yet closed --- and no
    project may ship with a red circle);
  \item \textcolor{orange!85!black}{\textbf{amber}} = ``you are here'' --- the
    pulsing marker of where to resume.
\end{itemize}
The dashboard is a manifestation of the checklist so that both the researcher's
future self \emph{and future Claude sessions} can always resume with full
knowledge of project state.

\subsection{Custom skills}

The workflow is extended with bespoke, invokable ``skills'' (slash-commands),
e.g.\ \texttt{/covariates} (proposes candidate covariates from a stated modelling
philosophy), \texttt{/referee2} (an adversarial reviewer pass), and
\texttt{/blindspot} (surfaces overlooked threats). Global preferences (tone,
concision, banned phrases) are pinned in a \texttt{CLAUDE.md} configuration file.

\section{Curating the ``Canon''}

Because agents generate an abundance of files, figures, tables, and code with
overlapping names and near-identical stories, the researcher needs an explicit
\concept{canonisation} process. Cunningham draws the analogy to the early church
selecting the biblical canon from a mass of circulating texts:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=1pt]
  \item \concept{Canon} --- the curated pipeline of code that produces the
    exhibits supporting the final manuscript.
  \item \concept{Apocryphal} --- tolerated but not part of the canonical
    pipeline.
  \item \concept{Heresy / deprecated} --- explicitly excluded.
\end{itemize}
The claim is that before AI agents researchers curated their work
\emph{unconsciously}; agentic volume forces this curation to become an
intentional, rule-governed step (in the spirit of Gentzkow and Shapiro's
guidance on code, directories, and version control). The goal: the exhibits you
keep are exhibits you \emph{chose}, and they mean what you think they mean.

\section{The Human Bottleneck: Diminishing Returns to Reading}

A subtler cost is cognitive. Using Claude Code means \emph{reading constantly}
--- the agent's reasoning, its explanations, and its output --- more, Cunningham
suspects, than he has ever read before. He analyses this with a demand curve:
reading has \concept{diminishing marginal returns}, and because the agent has
lowered the price of work, the researcher slides down the curve into the
low-value region ($X_2$), skimming diffs and disengaging.

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{Design responses (staying near high-value $X_1$):}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=1pt]
  \item Ask the agent to be \emph{concise} --- ``if I write 10 characters, write 9.''
  \item Shift \emph{modality}: prefer figures and in-terminal ASCII visuals over
    long prose, and oscillate deliberately between mediums.
  \item Treat overload as a \emph{failed design to be fixed}, not a reason to
    ``work harder'' --- while recognising that design-fixing has its own
    diminishing-returns demand curve.
\end{itemize}

\section{Publishing, Costs, and the Broader Shift}

Cunningham argues that agentic AI decouples \concept{research} from
\concept{publishing}: producing results and packaging them for dissemination
become two separate activities with independent economics. Falling marginal
costs raise total output but require the verification, harness, and canon
disciplines above to keep aggregate error under control. He frames this less as
a new toolset than as a \emph{paradigm} for how empirical work is organised ---
one still actively being discovered, with each researcher likely to build a
personal harness shaped by their own comparative advantage.

\section*{Key Takeaways}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Key Takeaways}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em, itemsep=2pt]
  \item Agents make \emph{machine time and human time near-substitutes},
    collapsing the cost of \emph{producing} cognitive output.
  \item The binding constraint moves from production to \concept{verification};
    the production--verification wedge is the central management problem.
  \item The researcher becomes the accountable \concept{``CEO of the kitchen''}
    --- director and verifier, not manual producer.
  \item \concept{AI Agent drift} (silent sample changes, file proliferation) is
    the signature failure mode.
  \item The countermeasure is a \concept{rigid harness}: a staged checklist, a
    colour-coded lock dashboard, custom skills, and \texttt{CLAUDE.md} config.
  \item Abundant output demands an explicit \concept{canonisation} process to
    separate canon from apocrypha/heresy.
  \item Human attention is finite: manage \concept{diminishing returns to
    reading} by demanding concision and shifting modality --- and fix the
    \emph{design}, not the effort.
\end{enumerate}
